# What to do when an organiser does not reply

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

You sent a clear, friendly email offering a guest lecture or a talk. A week has passed and nothing has come back. It is tempting to send another email, then another, or to try a colleague of the person you wrote to.

Don't. Silence is common, and it usually means busy, not no. This guide sets out a simple rule set: one follow-up to the same person, no going around them, and then a switch to parallel paths that do not depend on that one inbox. These are the same rules Spoke At uses when it sends outreach on a user's behalf.

## Key takeaways

- No reply usually means the email was missed, not rejected.
- Send one short follow-up to the same person, about a week after the first email, in the same thread.
- Never go around the person: do not write to their colleagues, their manager or the department inbox about the same course or event.
- After the follow-up, pause that contact until the next term or event cycle.
- Use the time on other paths: student societies, a different course, similar opportunities elsewhere.

## Why organisers do not reply

Most non-replies have ordinary causes:

- **Volume.** Microsoft's 2025 analysis of Microsoft 365 data reported that the average employee receives 117 emails a day [[1]](#ref-1). The analysis excluded education customers, so it does not describe university staff directly, but it gives a sense of how crowded a working inbox can be.
- **Timing.** An email that arrives in the first week of term or during exams competes with students, marking and admin.
- **Wrong person.** The named contact may have handed the course to someone else, or be on leave.
- **No decision yet.** The coordinator may like the idea but not know the schedule. Many people leave an email unanswered until they can give a real answer.
- **Filtering.** Messages from unknown senders can end up in a spam or "other" folder.

There is also a psychological trap on the sender's side. In studies by Flynn and Lake, people asking for help underestimated by as much as 50% how likely others were to agree to a direct request [[2]](#ref-2). In a separate experiment, Roghanizad and Bohns found that email requests to strangers succeeded far less often than face-to-face requests, about 10% against about 70%, even though the people asking expected both to work equally well [[3]](#ref-3). That study used students and the authors call it exploratory, so treat it as a pointer, not a rule. Together the findings suggest two things: email is easy to overlook, and a single polite reminder is reasonable. They do not suggest that more pressure helps.

## The one follow-up rule

**When:** about 7 days after your first email. If you sent it just before a holiday or an exam period, wait until that has passed.

**Where:** reply in the same email thread, so the original message sits underneath. Do not start a new thread with a new subject line.

**Who:** the same person, and only that person.

**What:** three or four lines. Restate the offer in one sentence, make it easy to say no, and ask one question.

> **Subject:** Re: Guest lecture offer for [course code] [course name]
>
> Hi [First name],
>
> I'm following up on my note below in case it got buried. I'd still be glad to give a short guest session on [topic area] in [course name], in [term] or a later term.
>
> If it doesn't fit this time, a quick "not now" is completely fine.
>
> Best,
> [Name]

This template is our own and is meant to be adapted. Write it in the same language as your first email.

### Why only one?

A second reminder rarely adds information. The person has now seen your name twice. If they want to reply, they know how. A third email starts to feel like pressure, and in academia and small professional communities people talk to each other. Being the speaker who gave up gracefully is worth more than one more chance at an answer.

## Never go around the person

When someone does not reply, it is tempting to write to their colleague, the head of department or the general department inbox. Don't, for these reasons:

- **It puts the person in an awkward spot.** Their colleague or manager will forward your message back to them, now with the added question of why they did not reply.
- **It rarely works.** The department inbox is usually handled by administrators who pass course questions to the course coordinator, which is where you started.
- **It damages trust.** Coordinators decide who comes into their teaching. Going around them signals that you will not respect their decisions once you are in the room.

There is one clear exception. If the person replies and says someone else handles guest sessions, write to that person and mention who referred you. That is a referral, not a detour.

An automatic out-of-office reply is not a referral. Note the return date, and follow up after it if you have not used your follow-up yet.

## What to do instead: parallel paths

Once you have sent your follow-up, stop waiting. Mark that contact as paused until the next term or the next edition of the event, and put your energy into routes that do not depend on that inbox.

### Student societies

Student societies run their own events and are often easier to reach than teaching staff. The student union at KTH, for example, lists dozens of student associations, grouped into categories such as career, networking and social impact, and tech and science [[4]](#ref-4). Many publish a contact address on their profile: the KTH AI Society's union page lists its email and website, and says it aims to connect students from all fields to artificial intelligence [[5]](#ref-5). Use a society's published role inbox or contact form, not individual students' personal emails. Contacting a society is not going around a teacher, because it is a separate organiser with its own events. Our guide on [how to speak at student societies](/en/speak-at-student-societies) covers the details.

### A different course

The same university may run another course where your topic fits, with its own coordinator. That is a separate opportunity, not a way round the first person. Keep it modest: one or two course contacts per university per term, never in the same week, and never about the same course.

### Alumni routes

If you studied at the university, check for an alumni speaker scheme. Stockholm University, for example, invites alumni with an idea for a talk to contact its alumni office, which tries to match speakers with a suitable student audience [[6]](#ref-6). Offer your expertise in general terms. Do not ask the alumni office to chase the coordinator who did not reply.

### Similar opportunities elsewhere

Look for the same type of course or event at other universities, business schools or associations. If one coordinator was a good fit, others teaching the same subject probably are too.

### Next term

Courses come back. Stockholm University's spring 2027 term runs from 18 January to 6 June 2027, split into periods that start on 18 January, 22 February, 29 March and 3 May [[7]](#ref-7). If the course is published again next term and still matches, write once more, as a fresh first email. Our guide to [the academic calendar for guest lecturers](/en/academic-calendar-guest-lectures) explains when to time it.

## If a late reply arrives

Sometimes a reply comes weeks later. Answer warmly and quickly, without any comment on the delay. The coordinator may now be planning next term, which is exactly when you want to be in their inbox.

Before you send any of this, check your first email was strong. Our [guest lecture email templates](/en/guest-lecture-email-templates) show what to include and what to leave out.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I wait before sending a follow-up email?
About a week is a sensible default. If your first email arrived just before a holiday, the start of term or an exam period, wait until that has passed. One follow-up is enough.

### How many follow-up emails should I send if there is no response?
One. After that, pause the contact until the next term or the next edition of the event. More reminders rarely change the outcome and can harm your reputation.

### Can I contact the head of department if the course coordinator does not reply?
No. Going around the person who decides puts them in an awkward position and rarely works. Only write to someone else if the coordinator refers you to them.

### Does no reply mean no?
Not necessarily. Many emails go unanswered because the person is busy, the timing is wrong or the message was filtered. Treat it as "not now" and try a fresh first email when the course or event comes round again.

### Should I call instead of emailing again?
Only if the person publishes a phone number for that kind of enquiry, and count the call as your one follow-up. Do not call colleagues or a switchboard to find another route to them.
