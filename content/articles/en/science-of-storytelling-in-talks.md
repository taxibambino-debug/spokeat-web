# The science of storytelling: why stories make talks memorable

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

"Tell a story" is standard advice in presentation training. You may also have heard bolder claims: that stories release particular brain chemicals, or make facts many times more memorable. Such claims often come without a source.

The research behind storytelling is real, and some of it is strong. It is also more specific, and more limited, than the slogans suggest. This article sets out what studies on narrative, memory and persuasion actually found, where the evidence stops, and how to use that knowledge in a guest lecture, panel or meetup talk.

## Key takeaways

- A 2021 meta-analysis of more than 33,000 participants found that stories were better understood and better remembered than expository texts, with a moderate average effect [[1]](#ref-1).
- The evidence base has limits: results vary a lot between studies, there are signs of publication bias, and few studies tested memory after a delay or used spoken material [[1]](#ref-1).
- When people are absorbed in a story ("transported"), they tend to adopt beliefs that fit the story and to argue against it less [[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3).
- Brain-imaging studies of storytelling are interesting but small. Treat claims about "brain syncing" or mirror neurons with care [[4]](#ref-4)[[5]](#ref-5).
- Stories and data do different jobs. Use a story to carry one point, then state the point plainly.

## What the research shows about stories and memory

The best single summary is a meta-analysis by Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta, published in *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* in 2021. They combined 150 effect sizes from 37 articles, with 33,078 participants in total. Their conclusion: "stories were more easily understood and better recalled than essays" [[1]](#ref-1).

The overall effect was a Hedges' g of 0.55, which the authors describe as just over half a standard deviation. For memory alone, the effect was larger (g = 0.72). For comprehension, it was g = 0.48. The difference between memory and comprehension was not statistically significant [[1]](#ref-1).

That is a meaningful effect. It is not a miracle. And it comes with limits that the authors state themselves:

- **High variation.** Results differed widely between studies (I² = 98%) [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Possible publication bias.** A statistical test suggested bias, mainly among memory studies [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Few delayed tests.** Only four studies tested memory after a delay [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Little listening.** Only eight studies used listening rather than reading, and only six used verbal tests [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Uneven controls.** Only 28% of effect sizes came from studies that controlled for content [[1]](#ref-1).

For speakers, the last three points matter most. Most of the evidence is about reading, tested soon afterwards. A live talk, remembered weeks later, is a less studied case. The authors also caution against forcing all information into narrative form [[1]](#ref-1).

## Why stories might be easier to remember

Mar and colleagues offer several explanations. They are reasoned accounts drawn from earlier research, not all directly tested in the meta-analysis [[1]](#ref-1):

- **Familiar structure.** Stories mirror how we experience life: events in time order, linked by causes, organised around goals.
- **Predictable grammar.** Stories follow a recognisable pattern. Expository texts vary more and often lack connecting words such as "because".
- **Inference from prior knowledge.** Listeners draw on what they already know to fill gaps in a story. Exposition often needs specialist knowledge the audience lacks.
- **Emotion.** Stories evoke emotion more readily, and emotional content tends to be better remembered.

The practical point: what helps is not the word "story" but the structure underneath. Causes, goals, sequence and stakes make information easier to follow and to recall.

## Stories and persuasion: narrative transportation

Psychologists Melanie Green and Timothy Brock introduced the idea of "transportation": absorption in a story that combines attention, mental imagery and feeling [[2]](#ref-2). In their experiments, readers who were more transported held beliefs more consistent with the story. They also spotted fewer "false notes" in the text, which the authors read as less counterarguing. When the researchers reduced transportation through instructions, story-consistent beliefs weakened too. Labelling the story as fact or fiction generally did not change the results [[2]](#ref-2).

In a 2024 review, Green and Appel summarise later work [[3]](#ref-3):

- A meta-analysis of 76 articles found that more transported people showed more narrative-consistent feelings, beliefs, attitudes and behaviours.
- Another meta-analysis found narratives were more effective than non-narratives at creating change over time in attitudes, intentions and behaviours, though not beliefs.
- Comparisons of stories with statistics are mixed. One meta-analysis found statistics influenced beliefs and attitudes more, while stories influenced intentions more.
- Distraction and secondary tasks reduce transportation.
- "Light touch" interventions tend to have small and short-lived effects.

The last two points are relevant on stage. A story competes with phones, slides and side conversations. A single anecdote in a 20-minute talk is a light touch.

## What brain studies can and cannot tell you

A widely cited study by Stephens, Silbert and Hasson scanned the brain of a speaker telling an unrehearsed real-life story, then scanned listeners hearing the recording. Activity in speaker and listeners was coupled across many brain areas, and the coupling disappeared when listeners did not understand the language. Stronger coupling went with better comprehension, and some listener regions even anticipated the speaker [[4]](#ref-4).

It is an elegant study. It is also small: one English-speaking speaker and 11 to 12 listeners in the main analysis. The main link between coupling and comprehension was only marginally significant [[4]](#ref-4).

Be equally careful with mirror neurons. A review by Dinstein and colleagues concluded that human studies had mostly used methods that cannot show the movement-selective responses found in monkeys, and that broad fMRI activity during observation "cannot, therefore, be used to test theories regarding the function of human mirror neurons" [[5]](#ref-5). Green and Appel also warn about reverse inference when interpreting brain measures of transportation [[3]](#ref-3).

In short: neuroscience suggests that understanding a speaker involves tracking and predicting them. It does not prove that any particular storytelling technique "syncs brains".

## How to use stories in a talk

The research points to a few practical rules.

### 1. Let one story carry one point
Choose the point first. Then pick the story that shows it. State the point plainly after the story, so the audience does not have to guess.

### 2. Build in cause, goal and stakes
For example, a hypothetical data engineer giving a guest lecture might describe a migration: what the team wanted (goal), what went wrong and why (cause), and what it cost (stakes). That structure does more for memory than drama.

### 3. Use stories and data together
Aristotle already paired argument with the example, which moves from one particular case to a similar one [[7]](#ref-7). Modern research suggests numbers and stories influence different outcomes [[3]](#ref-3). Give the key number, then the case that makes it concrete. See [ethos, pathos and logos for professionals](/en/ethos-pathos-logos) for how this fits the classical framework.

### 4. Choose a representative case
Dahlstrom, writing in *PNAS*, argues that narratives are persuasive and that this creates ethical duties. Communicators should decide whether their aim is comprehension or persuasion, keep the core facts accurate, and be aware that picking an unrepresentative example raises ethical concerns [[6]](#ref-6).

### 5. Never invent a story and present it as real
If you use an illustration, say it is hypothetical. Your credibility is worth more than one memorable anecdote.

### 6. Protect attention
Because distraction reduces transportation [[3]](#ref-3), do not tell your best story over a busy slide. Pause, remove clutter and let the story land.

For more on why audiences forget talks and how to structure for recall, read [how to make your talk memorable](/en/make-your-talk-memorable).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do people really remember stories better than facts?
On average, yes, in the research so far. A meta-analysis found stories were recalled and understood better than expository texts, with a moderate effect. Most studies used reading and short delays, so the size of the effect for live talks remembered weeks later is less certain.

### How long should a story be in a presentation?
Research does not give a fixed length. Keep it as short as it can be while still showing a goal, a cause and a consequence. In a short talk, one or two focused stories usually serve better than many anecdotes.

### Is it true that storytelling releases oxytocin?
You will see this claim often. We did not include it here because we could not verify it against sources that meet our standards for this article. Brain and hormone claims about storytelling should be treated with caution until they are well replicated.

### Can I use a hypothetical story?
Yes, if you say clearly that it is hypothetical. Research found that labelling a story as fact or fiction generally did not change how absorbed readers became. Honesty about the source protects your credibility.

### Should every presentation be a story?
No. The authors of the main meta-analysis caution against forcing all information into narrative form. Use stories where cause, sequence and stakes matter, and use clear exposition and data where they serve the audience better.
