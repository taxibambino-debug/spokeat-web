# Why speaking builds industry authority

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

Most articles on the benefits of public speaking say it builds confidence. That may be true, but it does not help you decide whether to spend an evening preparing a talk for a meetup or a morning at a business school.

Authority is more concrete than confidence. It is what other people can see and check: your name on a programme, a recording of you explaining your field, a university that invited you back, a board that already knows your thinking.

This article looks at four concrete outcomes of speaking, what the evidence says about each, and where that evidence is weak.

## Key takeaways
- Speaking creates checkable credentials: programme listings, recordings and, in some professions, formal credits.
- One talk leads to the next. Organisers ask for past talks, and universities actively look for practitioners.
- Board and senior roles are filled largely through networks. Speaking puts you in front of the people in those networks.
- A large survey sponsored by LinkedIn and Edelman found buyers trust expert content more than marketing material. It is self-reported data and not specific to talks.
- Authority builds through repetition on one clear topic, not through a single big stage.

## What "authority" means in practice

In a professional context, authority is a reputation that others can verify. When someone is deciding whether to hire you, invite you, buy from you or put you on a board, they look for evidence that you know your subject. A talk creates that evidence in public.

That is different from fame. A head of payments who has spoken at three chamber breakfasts and two university courses is not famous. But the people who matter in that field have seen them explain the subject clearly, and that is what authority is.

## Outcome 1: credentials you can point to

### Your name on someone else's programme
When an association, chamber or university lists you as a speaker, a third party has vouched for you. That counts for more than anything you write about yourself, and it is searchable.

### Formal credits in some professions
Some professional bodies treat speaking as professional development. ISACA's Sacramento chapter, for example, tells speakers they can "earn CPE credits for presenting" [[1]](#ref-1). If your field has continuing-education requirements, check whether your own body does the same.

### Proof for the next organiser
Organisers want evidence that you can speak. Mind the Product asks applicants who have spoken before to include a video of a past talk [[2]](#ref-2). Every talk you give makes the next application stronger. Our guide on [turning one talk into a month of content](/en/turn-a-talk-into-content) shows how to capture that proof.

## Outcome 2: invitations

Speaking compounds. Each talk puts you in front of people who organise other events, teach courses or run networks.

**Universities are actively looking.** Stockholm University says guest lecturers who can share real-world experience are "in high demand" from students, academic advisers and course leaders [[3]](#ref-3). KTH says "students value the opportunity to meet alumni with professional experience" and runs guest lectures together with its career service [[4]](#ref-4). In Sweden, universities also have a legal duty to collaborate with the wider society and to make sure their knowledge benefits it [[5]](#ref-5). Practitioners who speak in courses are one way they do that.

**Meetups feed conferences.** Mind the Product describes its local ProductTank meetups, held in over 200 cities, as a way for first-time speakers to build experience and become part of its speaker network, with its conferences as the larger stages [[2]](#ref-2).

The practical lesson: treat every talk as the first step to the next one. Ask the organiser at the end whether they know anyone else looking for speakers.

## Outcome 3: board and advisory roles

This is the outcome professionals ask about most, and it is where speaking connects most directly to networks.

Research on board appointments suggests that boards rarely recruit strangers. A study of 9,923 board appointments at US companies from 2003 to 2014, summarised by the authors on the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance, found that over 75% of new appointments came from a board's first- or second-degree network. For S&P 500 firms, 94% of nominees came from connected individuals, who made up only 26% of the director pool [[6]](#ref-6). The authors describe a market where "companies never advertise vacancies and candidates do not submit their applications" [[6]](#ref-6).

A smaller survey points the same way. In a 2017 online survey of 302 members of the Institute of Directors in Ireland, 42% had been appointed through a direct approach, against 19% through an independent process, and 67% knew up to three or more board members before joining [[7]](#ref-7).

**What this does and does not show.** These figures describe how boards recruit, not what speaking does. Neither source studies speakers. The US data covers listed companies, and the Irish figures come from a member survey. The reasonable conclusion is narrower: if board roles go mainly to people already known to the board, then being seen explaining your field to senior audiences (at chamber breakfasts, executive forums and industry associations) is one way to become known. Our guide on [speaking at industry associations and chambers](/en/speak-at-industry-associations-and-chambers) explains how to get on those programmes.

## Outcome 4: trust from buyers and partners

The most-cited data on this comes from the 2024 Edelman-LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report, an online survey of 3,484 business executives in seven countries, carried out from 30 November to 14 December 2023 [[8]](#ref-8).

Key findings:

- 73% of decision-makers said an organisation's thought leadership is a more trustworthy basis for assessing its capabilities than its marketing materials and product sheets [[8]](#ref-8).
- 86% said they would be moderately or very likely to invite an organisation that consistently produces high-quality thought leadership into an RFP process [[8]](#ref-8).
- 54% said organisations that consistently produce thought leadership had prompted them to research their offers [[9]](#ref-9).
- Only 15% rated the thought leadership they read as very good or excellent [[9]](#ref-9).

**The limits.** The report was produced by a PR firm and LinkedIn, and the respondents were LinkedIn members. The answers are self-reported. "Thought leadership" in the report is mostly content in general; it mentions webinars and live presentations only as examples of formats and has no findings specific to speaking [[8]](#ref-8). Treat it as a signal that expertise shown in public earns trust, not as proof that a talk wins deals.

The 15% figure is the more useful one. Most expert content is average. A specific, well-prepared talk stands out because so little does.

## What speaking does not do

Being honest about the limits helps you plan.

- **One talk is not enough.** The Edelman-LinkedIn findings are about organisations that produce thought leadership consistently [[9]](#ref-9). The same applies to people.
- **It is slow.** Invitations and board conversations come months or years after the talks that led to them.
- **Do not expect a fee.** None of the university, meetup and association pages cited here mention paying speakers. The return is visibility. Be wary of anyone who asks *you* to pay for a slot; read our guide to [paying to speak](/en/paying-to-speak).
- **It does not replace expertise.** Speaking makes real expertise visible. It cannot create it.

## How to make it compound

1. **Pick one topic you can own.** Three talks on the same subject build more authority than three talks on three subjects.
2. **Mix audiences.** A university course, an association breakfast and a meetup reach students, peers and senior buyers with the same expertise.
3. **Keep a speaking record.** List each talk, date, organiser and link on your profile. Our guide to [what organisers check in your speaker profile](/en/speaker-profile-what-organisers-check) explains what to include.
4. **Reuse every talk.** A recording, photos and a written version keep working after the event. See [turn one talk into a month of content](/en/turn-a-talk-into-content).
5. **Involve your employer.** Talks at universities also help your company's reputation with future hires. See [guest lectures and employer branding](/en/guest-lectures-employer-branding).

**Speaking and promotion.** If you are working towards a promotion, bring your speaking record to the conversation. It is evidence, from outside your team, that you can explain your field and that others want to hear it. We have not found research that measures whether speaking leads to promotion, so treat it as supporting evidence rather than a shortcut. Our guide on [building your personal brand through speaking](/en/personal-brand-through-speaking) explains how to make that record visible and consistent.

For where to start, read our guide on [how to get speaking opportunities](/en/how-to-get-speaking-opportunities).

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the career benefits of public speaking?
Concrete benefits include named credits on event programmes, recordings you can show, invitations to further events and access to senior networks. In some professions speaking also counts towards continuing professional education [[1]](#ref-1).

### Does public speaking help you get on a board?
There is no direct study of that. Research shows boards recruit mostly from people they already know [[6]](#ref-6) [[7]](#ref-7), and speaking to senior audiences is one way to become known to them.

### How many talks does it take to build authority?
There is no fixed number. Consistency on one topic matters more than the size of any single stage.

### Is thought leadership really trusted more than marketing?
In the Edelman-LinkedIn survey, 73% of decision-makers said so [[8]](#ref-8). It is self-reported data from LinkedIn members, so treat it as a strong signal rather than proof.

### Should I speak if I am not senior yet?
Yes. Meetups and guest lectures welcome practitioners at many levels [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3), and an early speaking record makes later invitations easier.
