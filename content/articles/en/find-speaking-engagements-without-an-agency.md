# How to find speaking engagements without a speaker agency

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

When professionals decide they want to speak more, many first think of a speaker agency. It seems logical: agencies know the organisers, so let them find the bookings.

The problem is that agencies are built for a different kind of speaker. They earn a share of the fee, so they represent people who already command substantial fees and have the video and testimonials to prove it. If you are a head of product, a CFO or a researcher who wants visibility, credibility and practice, you are usually not who they are looking for.

This article explains what agencies require, why that rarely fits professionals early in their speaking, and the route that does: doing the agent's core work yourself, in a structured way.

## Key takeaways
- Speaker agencies work on commission. One US bureau says it typically takes 25–35% of the fee and expects listed speakers to earn about $10,000 or more per engagement [[1]](#ref-1).
- Agencies ask for proof: video of recent talks, a list of past engagements and your fee [[2]](#ref-2).
- Many opportunities that build credibility, such as guest lectures, society talks and meetups, pay no fee or cover expenses only. That puts them outside an agency's business model.
- You can do the agent's core work yourself: find opportunities, check they are real, find the right contact, pitch well and follow up once.
- A simple tracking sheet and a steady weekly routine replace most of what an agent would do at this stage.

## What speaker agencies actually do

A speaker agency, or speakers bureau, matches clients who want a speaker with speakers it represents or lists. The client pays the speaker's fee, and the agency takes a share. BigSpeak, a US bureau, says its commission is "typically between 25-35% of the fee" and that it charges speakers nothing to be listed [[1]](#ref-1).

That model shapes who agencies take on. If the fee is zero, as it often is for guest lectures and meetup talks, a share of it is zero too.

Large agencies can also be very big. Talarforum, a Scandinavian speaker agency, says it represents more than 8,000 speakers [[3]](#ref-3). In a roster that size, a new speaker without a booking history competes for attention with established names.

## What agencies ask for

Here is what three agencies publish about the speakers they want.

| Agency | What they ask for or look for | Source |
|---|---|---|
| BigSpeak (US) | Video of on-stage appearances (a sizzle reel preferred), testimonials from large companies, evidence of steady demand, a book; listed speakers should already earn around $10,000+ per engagement | [[1]](#ref-1) |
| Leading Authorities (UK page) | Biography, speech topics with descriptions, video of recent speaking, speaking fee and travel requirements, list of speaking engagements in the past year | [[2]](#ref-2) |
| National Speakers Bureau (Canada and US) | Credibility as a representative of your field and the ability to "inspire, inform and entertain"; represents "a select number" of speakers | [[4]](#ref-4) |

Notice what every list assumes: that you already speak regularly, have recordings and charge a fee. Agencies are a way to scale an existing speaking business, not a way to start one.

## Why this rarely fits professionals

Most professionals who want to speak are not trying to earn a living from it. They want to be known in their field, to share what they know and to get better at presenting. The opportunities that serve those goals look different from agency work.

- **University courses and business schools** invite practitioners because students value real-world experience. In one survey of 114 undergraduates, students most valued industry speakers who currently worked in a related field, spoke honestly and answered questions [[5]](#ref-5). None of the university guest-speaker pages we checked mentioned a speaker fee.
- **Student societies** run their own speaker series, such as the Initiate lectures run by the Entrepreneurship Society at the Stockholm School of Economics [[6]](#ref-6).
- **Meetups** ask for speakers at all experience levels. AWS User Group Stockholm says "Speakers are always welcome!" [[7]](#ref-7).
- **Practitioner conferences** often cover travel or give a ticket rather than pay a fee. DevSum in Stockholm covers speakers' travel and accommodation and waives the event fee [[8]](#ref-8). brightonSEO gives speakers an all-access ticket and complimentary passes and does not mention fees [[9]](#ref-9).

These are exactly the venues that build the track record agencies later ask for. You reach them directly.

## The route for everyone else: do the agent's work yourself

An agent does five things: finds opportunities, judges fit, knows who to contact, pitches and follows up. You can do each one.

### 1. Find opportunities
Build a list of venues in six categories: university courses, student societies, alumni programmes, associations and chambers of commerce, meetups and conferences. Our [practical guide to getting speaking opportunities](/en/how-to-get-speaking-opportunities) explains each one, and the [2027 overview of speaking opportunities](/en/speaking-opportunities-2027) lists verified examples with deadlines.

If you are wondering how to get speaking gigs without an agency, start smaller than a keynote. Your first gigs are more likely to be a course lecture, a society evening or a meetup slot. They pay little or nothing, but they give you the references an agency would ask for later.

### 2. Qualify each one
Check three things before you pitch:
- **Evidence they host outside speakers.** A past events page, a speaker series or an explicit invitation. Stockholm University's alumni office, for example, says guest lecturers with real-world experience are "in high demand" [[10]](#ref-10).
- **Fit with your topic and audience level.**
- **Terms.** Does the organiser cover travel? Do they expect you to buy a ticket? If the page does not say, ask.

Drop anything you cannot confirm.

### 3. Find the right route
Use the contact route the organisation publishes: a speaker form, a functional inbox such as an alumni or events address, or the named role responsible, such as a course coordinator. For conferences, the official call for speakers is the only route. Sessionize, a common platform, notes that the email you enter is where the organiser will contact you about your submission [[11]](#ref-11), so use one you check.

### 4. Pitch well
A good pitch is short and specific: who you are, which course or event you mean, what you would cover and what the audience gets. Write in the organiser's language. See our [templates for emailing a course coordinator](/en/guest-lecture-email-templates).

### 5. Follow up once, then stop
Send one polite follow-up after a week or two. If there is no reply, move on. Do not write to colleagues of the person you contacted to get around them, and do not send a stream of pitches to the same organiser. That is how an agent protects relationships, and you should do the same.

## A simple tracking system

Agents keep track of every conversation. A spreadsheet does the same job.

| Column | What to record |
|---|---|
| Organiser | Name and link to the page that shows they host speakers |
| Type | Course, society, alumni, association, meetup, conference |
| Route | Form, inbox or role, with the source page |
| Deadline or timing | Call closing date, or the term or study period |
| Terms | Expenses covered, ticket, fee, or unknown |
| Pitch sent | Date |
| Follow-up sent | Date (one only) |
| Outcome | Yes, no, no reply, or try again next term |

Spend a fixed hour a week on it. Add five venues, send two or three pitches, and send the follow-ups that are due.

## When an agency might make sense

An agency becomes relevant when you already have what agencies ask for: recent video, a list of engagements, testimonials and a fee that clients pay. At that point an agency can help you reach clients you cannot reach alone. Until then, direct outreach builds the record that makes representation possible.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a speaker agency to get speaking engagements?
No. Most guest lectures, society talks, association seminars, meetups and many practitioner conferences are booked directly with the organiser. Agencies mainly handle paid engagements for established speakers [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2).

### How much do speaker agencies charge?
Models vary. BigSpeak says it typically takes 25–35% of the speaker's fee and does not charge to list speakers [[1]](#ref-1). Ask any agency for its terms in writing before you agree to anything.

### What do I need before an agency will consider me?
Agencies publish requirements such as recent video, a list of past engagements, topic descriptions and your fee [[2]](#ref-2). BigSpeak also mentions testimonials from large companies and evidence of steady demand [[1]](#ref-1).

### How do I find unpaid speaking engagements that are still worth doing?
Look for audiences that matter to your goals: students in a course related to your work, peers in an industry association or practitioners at a meetup. Check that the organiser hosts outside speakers and that the terms are clear before you pitch.
