# How to get invited to speak at industry associations and chambers of commerce

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

Industry associations and chambers of commerce run a steady stream of member events: breakfast seminars, lunch talks, roundtables and fireside chats. The audience is people in your own market, and the rooms are often small: one chamber's monthly breakfast typically draws 30 to 40 attendees [[6]](#ref-6). People remember a speaker in a room that size.

Most professionals never get on those programmes because they do not know how the slots are filled. They send a general "I'd love to speak" email and hear nothing back.

This guide explains how member events work, what organisers actually ask for, and why a narrow, timely topic gets a yes when a general offer does not.

## Key takeaways
- Associations and chambers run many short, recurring formats: breakfast seminars, lunch talks, roundtables, fireside chats and webinars.
- Many slots are filled by member companies that host the event and supply a speaker, so membership or a member contact often opens the door.
- Organisers that publish speaker guidelines ask for the same things: a topic relevant to their members, practical takeaways, real expertise and no sales pitch.
- A specific topic tied to something members face right now is far easier to say yes to than a general offer to "talk about leadership".

## What member events look like

Associations and chambers do not run one big conference and stop. They run a calendar.

AmCham Sweden's programme, for example, includes breakfast seminars, Executive Forum roundtables for senior leaders, fireside chats, webinars and receptions. Its listing shows an Economic Outlook breakfast on 18 November 2026 from 08:00 to 09:30 and a fireside chat on 19 January 2027 from 17:00 to 19:00 [[1]](#ref-1).

The British-Swedish Chamber of Commerce has a similar mix: Financial Outlook breakfasts from 07:30 to 09:00, fireside chats, geopolitical updates and roundtables. Each listing names both the speakers and a host, and many hosts are firms such as banks, law firms and consultancies [[2]](#ref-2).

Smaller chambers work the same way at a smaller scale. The Dutch Chamber of Commerce in Sweden held an "Early Breakfast seminar on AI" in May 2025 with two speakers from industry on how large language models affect productivity and workplace expectations [[3]](#ref-3). Mellansvenska Handelskammaren, which says it has more than 370 member companies, ran a free business-outlook lunch event hosted at a member company in November 2026 [[4]](#ref-4).

Chambers also co-host panels with partners. The Stockholm Chamber of Commerce and Stockholm International School ran an English-language seminar on attracting international talent, with a one-hour panel followed by networking [[5]](#ref-5).

### Common formats

| Format | Typical length | What the organiser needs |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast seminar | 60–90 minutes in total, talk inside that | One clear topic, practical takeaways, a speaker who can take questions |
| Lunch talk or member series | About 40 minutes plus Q&A | A session description with learning objectives |
| Roundtable or executive forum | 60–120 minutes | A discussion leader with a sharp question, not a lecture |
| Fireside chat | Usually an evening slot | A person with a point of view and a moderator to interview them |
| Panel | Part of a longer seminar | A distinct angle that the other panellists do not cover |

Lengths vary by organiser. Check the event page before you pitch.

## How slots actually get filled

There are three common routes. Knowing which one an organiser uses tells you who to contact.

**1. A member company hosts and supplies the speaker.** When a listing names a bank or law firm as host and one of its experts as the speaker, the slot came through membership [[2]](#ref-2). If your employer is a member, talk to the person who manages that membership. That person often has more pull than you think.

**2. The organiser runs an open call or speaker form.** Some chambers and association chapters publish guidelines and a form. The Mukilteo Chamber in the US asks for a topic "specifically relevant to business owners", gives 20 to 30 minutes of speaking time and has its board approve applications [[6]](#ref-6). The Asheville Area Chamber's form asks for a presentation overview, a bio, your available dates and key takeaways [[7]](#ref-7).

**3. The events team invites speakers directly.** Large chambers often curate their programmes. AmCham Sweden's programme page does not describe a process for members to propose speakers [[1]](#ref-1). In that case, the way in is a short, specific email to the events or programme team.

## What organisers ask for

Where organisers publish criteria, the same four points come up again and again.

**Relevance to members.** Mukilteo asks for a topic relevant to business owners [[6]](#ref-6). ISACA's Sacramento chapter wants "practical, practitioner-focused content aligned to ISACA domains" [[8]](#ref-8). An association serves a specific member group. Your topic must fit that group, not your own agenda.

**Practical takeaways.** Asheville asks for "specific, practical steps" that people can apply at work, and for the speaker to have real experience of the topic [[7]](#ref-7). ISACA asks for "clear learning objectives" [[8]](#ref-8).

**No selling.** Mukilteo states: "This is not an opportunity to sell your product or service" [[6]](#ref-6). Asheville bans "solicitation of products or services by the speaker" [[7]](#ref-7). ISACA asks for vendor-neutral education [[8]](#ref-8). If your pitch reads like a product demo, it will be declined.

**A bio and availability.** Asheville's form asks for your bio and the dates you can do [[7]](#ref-7). Have a short, current speaker bio ready before you pitch. Our guide to [what organisers check in your speaker profile](/en/speaker-profile-what-organisers-check) covers what to include.

## Why a specific topic pitch works

An events manager at a chamber has a calendar to fill and a membership to keep happy. A general offer ("I can talk about leadership or digital transformation") forces them to do the thinking. A specific topic does the thinking for them.

Look at what actually gets programmed. Företagarna's Lidingö branch in Sweden ran a breakfast on dividend rules and ownership planning after a tax rule change took effect on 1 January 2026, with an accountant as the speaker [[9]](#ref-9). The topic was narrow, current and useful to every small-business owner in the room.

A strong association pitch has five parts:

1. **A title that names the problem.** "What the new payment rules mean for mid-sized retailers" beats "The future of payments".
2. **Why now.** A new regulation, a market shift, recent data or a seasonal deadline.
3. **Who it is for.** Name the member group: finance leads, founders, HR managers.
4. **Three takeaways.** Concrete things people can do afterwards.
5. **Your proof.** One or two lines on why you know this from practice.

### A hypothetical example

For example, a head of compliance at a payments company might pitch a chamber's breakfast series like this:

> **Proposed session:** What changes for your invoicing when [named rule] takes effect
> **For:** Finance managers at member companies with cross-border customers
> **Takeaways:** a checklist of the three changes that matter; the questions to ask your bank; a timeline for the next six months
> **Format:** 25-minute talk plus Q&A, fits your breakfast format
> **No sales:** vendor-neutral, no product content

This takes the organiser two minutes to judge. That is the point.

## How to find the right associations

Start with the organisations closest to your daily work, then widen out.

- **Your own industry body.** Trade and professional associations for your sector, including local chapters.
- **Chambers of commerce.** Regional chambers and bilateral chambers (for example British-Swedish or American chambers) if your work has an international angle.
- **Business owners' networks.** Local branches of small-business organisations run frequent breakfast meetings with expert speakers [[9]](#ref-9).
- **Adjacent professions.** If you work in payments, finance and procurement associations may want you more than payments groups do.

For each one, open the events calendar and note three things: which formats they run, who hosts, and whether there is a speaker form. That tells you which of the three routes to use. ## Mistakes that get pitches ignored

- **Pitching a talk that is really a sales demo.** Organisers say no explicitly [[6]](#ref-6) [[7]](#ref-7).
- **Sending the same email to every association.** Mention their member group and their format.
- **Ignoring the calendar.** Programmes are published well ahead: in mid-November 2026, AmCham Sweden was already listing a January 2027 fireside chat [[1]](#ref-1). Pitch early in the planning cycle.
- **Following up too often.** One polite follow-up is enough. Our guide on [what to do when an organiser does not reply](/en/organiser-does-not-reply) explains how.
- **Paying to "speak" without realising it.** Some sponsorship packages include a speaking slot. That is advertising, not an invitation. Read our guide to [paying to speak](/en/paying-to-speak) first.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to be a member to speak at a chamber of commerce?
Not always. Some events are open to members and guests, and some chambers co-host with outside partners [[1]](#ref-1) [[5]](#ref-5). Many slots do come through member companies, though, so membership or a member contact makes it easier.

### Do associations pay speakers?
None of the event pages and speaker guidelines we checked mention a speaker fee. Treat it as a visibility opportunity unless the organiser says otherwise, and ask before you agree if a fee matters to you.

### How long is a typical breakfast seminar talk?
It varies. One chamber gives speakers 20 to 30 minutes inside a one-hour breakfast [[6]](#ref-6), while another lunch series runs about 40 minutes plus 10 minutes of questions [[7]](#ref-7). Check the specific event before you plan your content.

### Can I mention my company in the talk?
A brief introduction is normally fine. One chamber allows a short overview of your business but says the talk is "not an opportunity to sell" [[6]](#ref-6). Keep the content vendor-neutral.

### Who should I email at an association?
The events, programme or member-relations team. If a member company usually hosts, the contact person for that membership inside the company is a good first step.
