# Speaking opportunities for women and under-represented speakers

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

If you are a woman, or belong to another group that is rarely seen on stage, you may have noticed that the line-ups at events in your field do not look like the people doing the work. The research backs that up, at least for the fields that have been studied.

This guide summarises what the studies we opened show, with their limits. It lists networks and speaker directories we verified on their own pages, and explains how to use them and what organisers and colleagues can do.

## Key takeaways

- In a study of 98 medical conferences, women were 30.1% of 23,440 speakers, and 36.6% of panels had only men [[1]](#ref-1).
- At US university colloquia, men were more likely to be invited speakers even after accounting for the pool of available speakers. Men and women did not differ in how often they declined [[2]](#ref-2).
- In one study, the gap was widest in invited and keynote slots. Open calls, where anyone can submit, are a practical way in [[3]](#ref-3).
- We verified six networks and directories that help women get seen as speakers. That is a short list on purpose: we only include what we could check.
- Organisers can change the numbers. More women on planning committees, or chairing a series, was linked to more women speakers [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2).

## What the research shows about who gets to speak

Most published data on speaker representation comes from medicine and academia. We found no comparable study of Swedish business or tech conferences. Read the numbers below as evidence of a pattern, not as figures for your field.

**Medical conferences.** Arora and colleagues analysed 98 medical conferences across 20 specialties and five regions, held from March 2017 to November 2018. Of 23,440 speakers, 7,064 (30.1%) were women. Of 5,409 panels, 1,981 (36.6%) were men only, against 363 (6.7%) women only. The share of women varied widely, from 5.8% to 74.5% depending on region and specialty. Conferences with more women on the planning committee had more women speakers [[1]](#ref-1).

**University colloquia.** Nittrouer and colleagues studied 3,652 colloquium talks at 50 prestigious US universities in six disciplines in 2013 to 2014. Men were more likely than women to be speakers, even after accounting for the gender and rank of available faculty. Follow-up data showed that men and women valued speaking invitations equally and declined them at similar rates, which rules out "women turn invitations down" as the explanation. Where women chaired the colloquium, more women spoke. The authors describe those who invite speakers as gatekeepers [[2]](#ref-2).

**Invited versus submitted slots.** A study of 4,340 presentations at chiropractic research conferences from 2010 to 2019 found that women gave 35% overall. The share rose from 30% in 2010 to 42% in 2019. But women gave 21% of invited and keynote presentations, against 41% of presentations selected from submitted abstracts [[3]](#ref-3).

**The limits.** The chiropractic study, for example, inferred gender from names, photos and online biographies, and could not identify gender-diverse people [[3]](#ref-3). They cover medicine, chiropractic and US universities, not business events in Sweden. And they say nothing about ethnicity, disability, age or other dimensions. We did not find studies on those for speaker line-ups that we could open and check, so we make no claims about them.

## What this means for you

Two findings are useful in practice.

First, the gap seems smallest where the selection is most open. If invited slots go to people organisers already know, a [call for speakers](/en/call-for-speakers-guide) lets your proposal be judged on its content. It is not a guarantee, but it is a way around the gatekeeping the studies describe.

Second, the person who decides matters. Both the medical and the university studies found more women speakers where women were part of the planning or chairing [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2). When you research a venue, look at who sits on the programme committee. That tells you whom to approach.

## Events that state a diversity goal

Some calls for speakers say outright that they want a broader line-up. Øredev, the developer conference in Malmö, states "a strong emphasis on diversity and inclusion" and a goal of "promoting gender equality and supporting under-represented groups in the tech industry". It also covers speakers' travel and accommodation, up to three hotel nights. Its call for the November 2026 conference ran in March 2026 [[14]](#ref-14).

A stated goal does not tell you how selection works, but covered costs remove one real barrier. Read any call's terms before you submit, and see our guide on [paying to speak](/en/paying-to-speak) if a fee is involved.

## How to use these routes

1. **Create one profile in a directory that fits your field.** A directory only works if organisers can find you under a clear topic. Use the same title and bio you use elsewhere. Our guide to [what organisers check in your speaker profile](/en/speaker-profile-what-organisers-check) explains what to include.
2. **Go to a network event, then offer a session.** A members' meet-up is a small, friendly room. After one or two events, offer a short talk on something specific from your work.
3. **Apply to open calls.** Given the research above, open calls are worth more of your time than waiting for an invitation.
4. **Say yes to a panel.** A panel is a shorter first step than a solo talk. See our [guide for panellists and moderators](/en/panellist-and-panel-moderator-guide).
5. **Use the general method too.** Specialist networks are one route among several. Our [practical guide to getting speaking opportunities](/en/how-to-get-speaking-opportunities) covers university courses, associations and meetups.

If nerves or doubts about whether you are "expert enough" hold you back, read [shy, introverted or afraid of speaking](/en/shy-introverted-afraid-of-speaking), which includes a section on impostor feelings.

## What organisers and colleagues can do

The studies point to the people who choose speakers, so most of the levers sit with them.

- **Broaden the committee.** More women on planning committees, or chairing a series, was linked to more women speakers [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2).
- **Check the panel before you publish it.** In 2018, EU Commissioner Mariya Gabriel launched the "No Women No Panel" campaign and committed to making sure at least one other woman speaks on every panel or public event she is invited to. Other Commissioners backed it [[4]](#ref-4).
- **Search directories, not only your contacts.** The directories above exist so organisers can find speakers outside their own network.
- **Run an open call with clear criteria,** and say whether you cover travel.
- **If you are invited and the line-up is narrow, suggest a name.** A short recommendation of a qualified colleague costs you little and may be the introduction they need.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are there speaker directories for women?
Yes. Speakerinnen.org lets women register as speakers and moderators for free, Innovation Women runs a database of women experts and speakers, and SheSpeaksCyber covers cybersecurity [[8]](#ref-8) [[6]](#ref-6) [[7]](#ref-7). Pick one that fits your field and keep the profile current.

### How many conference speakers are women?
It depends on the field, and most data comes from medicine and academia. In one study of 98 medical conferences, women were 30.1% of speakers [[1]](#ref-1). We found no comparable study of Swedish business or tech events.

### Is it better to apply to open calls or wait for invitations?
Apply. In one study, women gave 41% of presentations chosen from submitted abstracts but only 21% of invited and keynote presentations [[3]](#ref-3). Open calls let your proposal speak for itself.
