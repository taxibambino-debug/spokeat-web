# After your talk: feedback, thanks and the next invitation

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

The talk is over. The room has emptied, the course coordinator has thanked you, and you feel relieved. Most speakers stop here. That is a missed chance, because the week after a talk is when the organiser remembers you best.

What you do in the next seven days decides whether this was a one-off or the start of a relationship: a repeat invitation, an introduction to a colleague, a quote for your speaker profile and an honest view of what to improve.

This article gives you a 7-day checklist, the research behind the most important steps, and a short thank-you email you can adapt.

## Key takeaways
- Send a short thank-you within a day or two. Research suggests people underestimate how much a thank-you note is appreciated.
- Ask for advice rather than feedback. Asking "what should I do differently next time?" gets more concrete answers than "any feedback?".
- Ask permission before you quote the organiser or publish photos that show other people.
- Ask for one referral. People tend to underestimate how often others say yes to a direct request.
- Write down what to change while you still remember, and log the talk in your speaking record.

## The 7-day checklist

| Day | Action | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Day 0 to 1 | Thank the organiser | Gratitude is valued more than senders expect |
| Day 1 | Write your own notes | Memory fades fast |
| Day 1 to 2 | Share the materials you agreed to share | Keeps your promise and makes you easy to work with |
| Day 2 to 3 | Ask for advice on what to improve | Gets concrete, usable input |
| Day 3 to 5 | Ask permission for a quote, and post with consent | Turns the talk into evidence |
| Day 5 to 7 | Ask for one referral | Your best route to the next invitation |
| Day 7 | Log the talk | Builds your speaking record |

You can combine some steps in one email. Do not send seven separate messages.

## Day 0 to 1: say thank you

Send a short, specific thank-you within a day or two. Thank the person who invited you, and anyone who helped on the day, such as a technician or a student who met you at reception.

**Why it is worth it.** In a series of experiments, Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley asked people to write gratitude letters and predict how the recipients would feel. Senders underestimated how happy recipients would be and overestimated how awkward they would feel. They also underestimated how competent and warm the recipients would judge them to be [[1]](#ref-1). The letters in the study were personal gratitude letters, not work emails after a talk, so the finding is a guide rather than a direct test.

### A short thank-you email you can adapt

> **Subject:** Thank you for [date]
>
> Hi [first name],
>
> Thank you for inviting me to speak to [course / group] on [date]. I enjoyed [one specific thing, for example a student's question or the discussion afterwards].
>
> As promised, here are [the slides / the reading list / the link]. Please share them with the group if that is useful.
>
> If you have a moment, I would value your advice: what is one thing I should do differently next time?
>
> Best regards,
> [Name]

Keep it this short. One specific detail shows you were paying attention. The question at the end is optional in the first email; you can ask it a few days later instead.

## Day 1: write your own notes

Before you ask anyone else, write down what you noticed. Spend ten minutes on:

- Which questions came up, and which ones you struggled with.
- Where the room lost attention, and where it leaned in.
- Timing: did you finish early, late or on time?
- One thing to keep and one thing to change.

These notes will be more useful than anything you remember in three months.

## Day 1 to 2: share what you agreed

If you promised slides, a reading list or a link, send it now. Only share what you agreed with the organiser beforehand. Some course coordinators want materials uploaded to their learning platform rather than sent to students directly. Ask if you are unsure.

## Day 2 to 3: ask for advice, not feedback

"Any feedback?" usually gets a polite "it was great". That is pleasant but useless.

A 2025 study in *Management Science* tested a simple change. In a preregistered field experiment with over 27,000 comments and two laboratory studies with 806 participants, people gave more concrete and actionable input when asked for "advice" rather than "feedback" [[2]](#ref-2). Asking for advice made people think about the future and what could be done differently [[3]](#ref-3). Independent raters judged the more concrete input as more useful [[2]](#ref-2).

**The limits.** The study was done in workplace reviews, not after talks. The advice framing worked best for improvement points and less well for describing strengths, and the researchers did not measure whether people acted on the advice [[3]](#ref-3).

Two or three simple questions are enough:

1. What is one thing I should do differently next time?
2. Was the level right for this group?
3. Did anything come up afterwards, from students or members, that I should know about?

If the organiser runs their own evaluation, ask whether they can share any comments about your session. Do not ask them to run a survey for you.

## Day 3 to 5: ask permission for a quote, and post with consent

A short quote from an organiser is strong evidence on your [speaker profile](/en/speaker-profile-what-organisers-check). Always ask first.

- **Ask for permission to quote something they already said.** If the organiser wrote "the students really appreciated your examples", ask whether you may use that sentence with their name and role.
- **Do not write the quote for them** and do not edit their words beyond small cuts they approve.
- **Check photos.** Photos of people are personal data under GDPR [[4]](#ref-4). Before you post pictures that show students or attendees, ask the organiser what is allowed.
- **Tag with care.** Ask before you tag the organiser or the institution on LinkedIn, and whether they have guidelines on how they want to be mentioned.

Our guide on [turning one talk into a month of content](/en/turn-a-talk-into-content) covers recordings, photos and what to post.

## Day 5 to 7: ask for one referral

The best source of your next talk is often the person who just heard your last one. Ask for one specific introduction, not "anyone you know".

For example: "Do you know another course coordinator or association in [field] who might be interested in a session like this?"

Many people do not ask because they expect a no. Research by Francis Flynn and Vanessa Lake found that people underestimated how likely others were to agree to a direct request for help by as much as 50% [[5]](#ref-5). Some institutions also invite this directly. Stockholm University's alumni page, for example, asks alumni to suggest other alumni it could connect with [[6]](#ref-6).

Keep the request light and easy to decline. If you do not get a reply, follow up once at most. Our guide on [what to do when an organiser does not reply](/en/organiser-does-not-reply) covers timing and tone.

## Day 7: log the talk

Add the talk to a simple record:

- Date, organiser, contact person and audience size.
- Topic and format.
- Links: programme page, recording, post.
- What you learned and what you will change.
- Next step: a referral, a repeat date or nothing yet.

This record feeds your speaker profile and helps you plan. Our [12-month plan from first talk to keynote](/en/first-talk-to-keynote-12-month-plan) shows how one talk leads to the next.

## How to get invited back

Repeat invitations usually come from three things: being easy to work with, being relevant to the course or audience, and staying in touch without pushing.

- **Be easy to work with.** Arrive on time, keep to your slot and send what you promised.
- **Offer, do not ask.** Near the end of your thank-you or follow-up, you can say you would be happy to come back if it fits the course. Leave it there.
- **Stay in touch lightly.** If you see something relevant to their course or field months later, send it with a short note. One message per term is plenty.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I send a thank-you email after a guest lecture?
Yes. Keep it short and send it within a day or two. Research suggests people who send thank-you notes underestimate how much they are appreciated [[1]](#ref-1).

### How do I ask for feedback after a presentation?
Ask one or two specific questions, framed as advice: "What is one thing I should do differently next time?" Research found that asking for advice produces more concrete input than asking for feedback [[2]](#ref-2).

### Can I quote the organiser on my website or LinkedIn?
Only with permission. Ask whether you may use their exact words with their name and role, and do not write the quote for them.

### How do I get invited back to speak?
Be easy to work with, send what you promised, ask for advice on how to improve and let the organiser know you would be happy to return. Then stay in touch lightly.

### Is it appropriate to ask the organiser for a referral?
Yes, if you ask for one specific type of introduction and make it easy to say no. People tend to underestimate how often others agree to direct requests [[5]](#ref-5).
