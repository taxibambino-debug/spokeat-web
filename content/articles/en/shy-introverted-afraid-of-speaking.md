# Shy, introverted or afraid of speaking in front of a group? How to start anyway

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)


You have expertise worth sharing. Maybe a colleague has suggested you give a talk, or a lecturer has asked if you would visit their course. Part of you wants to say yes. Another part would rather do almost anything else.

If you think of yourself as shy, introverted, or simply afraid of speaking in front of a group, this guide is for you. It explains how these three things differ, why the difference matters, and how to start with small, manageable steps. It also explains when the fear may be more than shyness, and where to get help.

This is general information, not a diagnosis or treatment advice.

## Key takeaways
- Shyness, introversion and anxiety are different things. You can be one without the others.
- Most shy people do not have social anxiety disorder. In one large US survey of teenagers, about half called themselves shy, but only 12% of those met criteria for social phobia.
- Shy speakers tend to judge their own performance more harshly than audiences do.
- A small-step path (a student society or meetup, then a panel, then a longer talk) lets you build experience without starting on the biggest stage.
- If the fear is strong, persistent and holds you back, talk to a GP or healthcare provider.

## Shy, introverted or anxious: what is the difference?

These words are often used as if they meant the same thing. They do not.

**Shyness.** The American Psychological Association (APA), drawing on its *Dictionary of Psychology*, describes shyness as "anxiety and inhibition in social situations". This can include physical signs such as a pounding heart or blushing, "worries about being evaluated negatively by others", and behaviour such as quietness and avoiding eye contact [[1]](#ref-1).

**Introversion.** Introversion is a personality trait about preference, not fear. An APA *Monitor on Psychology* article on shyness in children draws the line this way: introverted children prefer spending time alone, while shy children want to interact with others but find those interactions overwhelming [[2]](#ref-2). The article focuses on children, but the distinction is a useful starting point for adults too.

**Social anxiety.** The NHS describes social anxiety disorder as "a long-term and overwhelming fear of social situations" and makes clear that it is more than shyness [[3]](#ref-3). For some people, the anxiety "may arise only during performance situations such as giving a speech", according to the US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) [[4]](#ref-4).

| | Shy | Introverted | Social anxiety |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core feature | Feels tense or awkward in social encounters | Prefers less social stimulation | Strong, lasting fear of being judged |
| Wants contact? | Often yes | Often prefers less of it | Often yes, but fear gets in the way |
| Is it a disorder? | No | No | Can be, if assessed by a professional |
| Typical effect on speaking | Nerves, especially at first | May find it draining rather than frightening | May avoid speaking altogether |

Two cautions. First, these categories overlap, and you may recognise yourself in more than one column. Second, the table is a simplification for orientation. Only a qualified professional can assess whether you have an anxiety disorder.

## Being shy does not mean something is wrong

Shyness is common, and it is not a disorder. "Being shy is not a disorder," as Koraly Pérez-Edgar of Penn State told the APA *Monitor* [[2]](#ref-2).

The numbers back this up. In a nationally representative US survey of more than 10,000 teenagers, about half described themselves as shy. Only 12% of those shy teens met criteria for social phobia at some point in their lives, compared with about 5% of teens who did not call themselves shy [[5]](#ref-5). NIMH summarised the study as showing that social phobia is not simply shyness, and that it affects only a fraction of people who consider themselves shy [[5]](#ref-5).

**Limits:** the study covered US teenagers aged 13 to 18, and shyness was measured with a single self-rating question [[5]](#ref-5). Adult rates may differ.

A smaller doctoral study at the University of Maryland reached a similar conclusion, noting that "the vast majority of shy persons do not meet diagnostic criteria for social phobia" [[6]](#ref-6).

The reverse mistake also happens. A clinical review in the Swedish medical journal Läkartidningen notes that social phobia is often taken for ordinary shyness that will pass, which is one reason few people seek help. The authors add that prospective studies suggest few improve on their own [[10]](#ref-10). If your "shyness" is strong and holds you back, it is worth getting it assessed.

## You are probably harder on yourself than your audience is

In that University of Maryland study, shy participants, with and without social phobia, rated their own speech performance lower than independent observers did [[6]](#ref-6). It was a small study (78 people) and a dissertation rather than a journal article, so treat it as a pointer.

Research on the "illusion of transparency" points the same way. Speakers tend to overestimate how visible their nervousness is to the audience, and simply being told this can reduce the effect [[7]](#ref-7).

In practice: the talk that felt shaky to you may have looked fine from the third row.

## A small-step path to your first talk

Fear tends to grow when you avoid what you fear. Sweden's public health portal 1177 notes that the more you try to avoid what you are afraid of, the stronger the anxiety tends to become, and suggests practising in easier situations before gradually increasing the difficulty [[8]](#ref-8). A 2019 meta-analysis of 30 randomised controlled trials also found that structured psychological interventions reduce the fear of public speaking [[9]](#ref-9). Practising on your own is not therapy, but the same principle of gradual steps can guide how you plan your first talks.

Here is one way to build up:

1. **Speak where you already belong.** A team meeting, an internal lunch-and-learn or a professional group you know. Five minutes is enough.
2. **Find a friendly external audience.** [Student societies](/en/speak-at-student-societies) and [local meetups](/en/meetup-talks-first-slot) often welcome practitioners and tend to be informal.
3. **Join a panel before you give a keynote.** On a panel you share the stage, answer questions, and do not need to carry a whole session.
4. **Try a short guest lecture.** A 20-minute slot in a university course gives you a focused, curious audience.
5. **Then go longer.** Once shorter formats feel manageable, aim for a full talk or a conference slot.

*Hypothetical example:* a data analyst who dreads presenting might start with a five-minute demo at an internal meeting, then give a short talk at a local data meetup, then join a panel at a student society event before taking on a 45-minute guest lecture.

If you want structured practice with feedback, a [public speaking course](/en/public-speaking-courses-sweden) can be another step on the path.

## Tips that suit shy and introverted speakers

These are practical suggestions, not research findings:

- **Prepare well, but do not script every word.** 1177 lists preparing lines in advance as a "safety behaviour" that can keep anxiety going [[8]](#ref-8). Know your structure and key points instead.
- **Choose interactive formats if they suit you.** Some people find a Q&A or workshop easier than a monologue. Others find the opposite. Try both.
- **Plan recovery time.** If you are introverted, speaking may tire you even when it goes well. Leave space in your diary afterwards.
- **Arrive early.** Meeting a few audience members before you start can make the room feel smaller.
- **Ask a friend for honest, specific feedback.** Given the research above, their view is likely to be kinder and more accurate than your own.

For techniques on the day, read [research-based ways to calm nerves before a talk](/en/calm-nerves-before-a-talk). To understand the physical reaction, see [what happens in your body and brain when you fear public speaking](/en/fear-of-public-speaking-body-and-brain).

## Impostor feelings before a talk

Being invited to speak can bring a specific worry: "Why me? Someone will notice I do not really know enough." This is often called impostor syndrome or the impostor phenomenon. It is not the same as shyness.

**What the research says, and its limits.** A systematic review in the *Journal of General Internal Medicine* looked at 62 studies with 14,161 participants [[11]](#ref-11). Reported rates varied from 9% to 82%, largely depending on which screening tool and cut-off each study used. That spread tells you the measurement is uncertain, not how common the feeling really is. The review found that impostor feelings often occur together with depression and anxiety, and are linked to burnout and lower job satisfaction. At the time of the review, no published studies had evaluated treatments [[11]](#ref-11).

A later scoping review found 31 publications on interventions, mostly coaching, peer or support groups, and workshops [[12]](#ref-12). Some studies reported lower impostor scores, but only four were randomised controlled trials. Most participants were women in healthcare or higher education, so the results may not apply to everyone [[12]](#ref-12).

**It is not a diagnosis.** The systematic review states that impostor syndrome is not a recognised psychiatric disorder: it is not in DSM-5 or ICD-10 [[11]](#ref-11). Feeling like an impostor before a talk does not mean something is wrong with you. Equally, a label you find online cannot tell you whether something else, such as anxiety or low mood, is going on.

**Practical steps that many speakers find helpful** (suggestions, not research-tested treatments):

- **Remember why you were asked.** The organiser chose your experience for this audience. You do not need to know everything about the topic, only what you said you would cover.
- **Write down your evidence.** List the projects, decisions and results your talk is based on. Read the list when the doubt shows up.
- **Say "I do not know" in Q&A.** It is an honest answer, and you can offer to follow up by email.
- **Talk to someone.** A colleague who has spoken before, or a peer group, can put the feeling in proportion. Group support is one of the approaches the scoping review identified [[12]](#ref-12).

If impostor feelings come with persistent anxiety, low mood or exhaustion, or stop you from doing your job, treat that as a reason to talk to a professional, as described below.

## When it may be more than shyness

Consider talking to a GP or other healthcare provider if:

- the fear is strong and persistent and does not ease with practice;
- you avoid work, study or social opportunities because of it;
- you have panic attacks;
- you use alcohol or other substances to cope;
- you feel low or hopeless.

The NHS says it is a good idea to see a GP if you think you have social anxiety, and in the UK you can refer yourself to NHS talking therapies [[3]](#ref-3). NIMH suggests talking to a health care provider such as a primary care doctor, psychologist or psychiatrist [[4]](#ref-4). If you are in immediate danger or thinking about suicide, call your local emergency number. In the US, NIMH lists the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline [[4]](#ref-4).

Read more in [when the fear of speaking needs professional help](/en/when-fear-of-speaking-needs-help).

## Frequently asked questions

### Why am I afraid of speaking in front of a group?
Speaking in front of others means being visible and possibly judged, which triggers the body's stress response in many people. For shy people, that reaction can feel especially strong. If the fear is intense and lasting, a healthcare provider can help you understand it.

### Is being shy the same as being introverted?
No. Shyness is feeling tense or awkward in social situations. Introversion is a preference for less social stimulation. You can be an introvert who is not shy, or a shy person who wants more social contact.

### Can introverts be good public speakers?
Introversion describes a preference, not a skill level, so it does not rule out speaking well. Introverts may want more recovery time afterwards, and can choose formats and audiences that suit them.

### How do I know if my shyness is social anxiety?
Shyness on its own is common and not a disorder. Social anxiety involves a strong, persistent fear that gets in the way of daily life. Only a qualified professional can assess this, so talk to your GP or healthcare provider if you are unsure.

## Start small: find a friendly first audience

You do not have to start with a keynote. A student society, a meetup or a short guest lecture is a gentler first step, and each one makes the next easier.
