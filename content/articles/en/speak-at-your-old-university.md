# Speaking at your old university: how to go back as a guest speaker

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

You once sat in those lecture halls wondering what work would actually be like. Now you know. Going back to tell the next cohort is one of the most satisfying talks you can give, and one of the easiest to land.

Your old university is a natural first stage. You know the campus, the programmes and the culture. The staff are often more open to a former student than to a stranger. And several universities now say in public that they want alumni in the classroom.

This guide covers the three practical routes back: the alumni office, a course in your field, and student societies. It explains how each route works, what to send and what to expect.

## Key takeaways
- Universities actively ask for alumni speakers. Stockholm University says guest lecturers with real-world experience are "in high demand" [[1]](#ref-1).
- There are three routes in: the alumni office, the teacher of a specific course, and student societies. Use the one that matches your goal.
- Students value speakers who work in the field, speak honestly and answer questions [[2]](#ref-2). That is what an alumnus brings.
- Expect to teach, not to sell. Some schools tell guests to focus on course content rather than company presentations [[3]](#ref-3).
- Guest lectures are usually unpaid. Ask about expenses before you agree.

## Why your old university is a good place to start

Universities need people from working life. In Sweden, the Higher Education Act states that collaboration with the surrounding society is part of a university's task [[4]](#ref-4). Guest lectures are one everyday form of that collaboration.

Alumni offices say so directly. Stockholm University invites alumni to share their professional expertise with students and writes that such guest lecturers are in high demand from both students and academic advisors [[1]](#ref-1). KTH runs guest lectures together with KTH Career Service, where alumni talk about their working lives and career journeys [[5]](#ref-5). Uppsala University lists "Become a guest lecturer" as one way for alumni to get involved [[6]](#ref-6).

There is also evidence that students value this. In a survey of 114 undergraduates, 86.1% of those who had taken a class with an industry guest speaker said they liked the experience. They valued guests who currently worked in a related field, spoke with enthusiasm and candour, and answered questions. They particularly liked hearing about the speaker's daily work and getting career advice [[2]](#ref-2). The study is small and comes from one subject area (fashion), so read it as an indication. But it describes what most alumni can offer without extra preparation.

## The three routes back

| Route | Best for | Who you contact | Typical format |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alumni office | Career stories, panels, a first talk | The alumni team, often through a form or shared inbox | Guest lecture, alumni panel, fireside chat |
| A course in your field | Subject expertise, repeat invitations | The teacher or course coordinator | Guest lecture inside a scheduled session |
| Student societies | Fast bookings, informal talks | The society board or events team | Talk, panel, lunch or breakfast lecture |

### Route 1: the alumni office

This is the easiest door because it exists for exactly this purpose. Many alumni teams keep a list of people willing to speak and match them with programmes.

Some examples, checked on 16 November 2026:

- **Stockholm University** asks alumni to get in touch about the topic they want to present and says it will try to match them with a suitable student audience. The contact address is alumni@su.se [[1]](#ref-1).
- **KTH** has an online form where alumni register their interest in guest lecturing. Questions go to alumni@kth.se [[5]](#ref-5).
- **Uppsala University** lists guest lecturing alongside mentoring, graduation speeches and providing real case examples for programmes. The alumni team is at info@alumni.uu.se [[6]](#ref-6).
- **Hanken School of Economics** in Finland invites alumni to offer guest lectures to both students and other alumni via alumni@hanken.fi [[7]](#ref-7).
- **The University of Edinburgh Business School** lists alumni panels, on-course speaking and career lounges, both in person and online. It asks alumni to email the alumni team with their area of expertise [[8]](#ref-8).

The alumni office is a good route if you want a first talk, a career-focused session or a panel. The trade-off is control. The office decides when and where to place you, and it can take time.

### Route 2: a course in your field

If you have specific expertise, go to the course where it belongs. A course coordinator who needs a practitioner on payments, supply chains or product management is the person most likely to say yes, and to invite you back next year.

Universities point to this route themselves. KTH tells companies they can contact the teacher of a specific programme to offer a guest lecture [[9]](#ref-9). The University of Edinburgh calls it "on-course speaking" and frames it as sharing specialist knowledge from your field [[8]](#ref-8).

How to do it:

1. **Check the current course catalogue.** The course you took may have changed name, content or teacher. Do not rely on memory.
2. **Read the course description and schedule.** Find the week where your topic fits.
3. **Write to the course coordinator.** Say that you are an alumnus, name the course, and propose one session topic linked to a specific part of the syllabus. Our guide on [how to become a guest lecturer at a university](/en/become-a-guest-lecturer-at-a-university) walks through this in more detail.
4. **Mention flexibility.** Offer a short slot or a Q&A as well as a full lecture.

Timing matters. Teachers often plan sessions before term starts, so write well ahead of the course dates.

### Route 3: student societies

Societies are run by students, book their own events and often move faster than departments. They are also where students go for career talks. At the Stockholm School of Economics, the student Entrepreneurship Society runs a lecture series called Initiate in which entrepreneurs speak to students, and its list of past speakers includes SSE alumni [[10]](#ref-10).

Societies are a good fit for informal formats such as panels, breakfast talks or fireside chats. Read our guide to [speaking at student societies](/en/speak-at-student-societies) for how their boards and booking cycles work.

## What to send

Keep the first message short. A course coordinator or alumni officer should understand your offer in under a minute. Include:

- **Your link to the university.** Programme and graduation year.
- **What you do now.** Role, sector, and one concrete detail.
- **One topic, linked to students' needs.** For a course: the part of the syllabus it supports. For the alumni office: whether it is a career talk or a subject talk.
- **Format and length.** For reference, Sheffield University Management School says its guest sessions usually last 30 to 40 minutes plus 15 to 20 minutes for questions [[11]](#ref-11).
- **Your availability.** A range of weeks, not a single date.

For example, a hypothetical head of payments who graduated in economics ten years ago might write to the coordinator of a banking course. She would offer a 40-minute session on how instant payments changed her bank's operations, tied to the course module on payment systems, and say she is available during any week of the spring term.

## What to expect

**No fee, usually.** None of the alumni pages we checked mention payment. Aalto University School of Business states plainly that the school "does not pay a fee to the external participants" [[3]](#ref-3). Treat the talk as a way to give back and to build visibility. Ask about travel expenses if you live far away.

**Teaching, not marketing.** Aalto also asks guests to focus on course content rather than a company presentation [[3]](#ref-3). If your employer wants recruitment exposure, a student society event or a career fair is the right place for that.

**No guarantee.** Alumni offices match speakers to courses that are actually running. Aalto notes that it may not be able to take up every proposal because only a limited number of courses run at a time [[3]](#ref-3).

**A slow first reply.** Alumni teams and teachers have busy terms. One polite follow-up after a week or two is fine. If there is still no answer, try a different route rather than going around the person.

## Making it more than a one-off

A good first lecture often leads to a second. After the session:

- Thank the teacher and ask whether the topic should come back next year.
- Offer the alumni office a short note on how it went, so they can match you again.
- Ask whether a student society in your field would like a follow-up talk.
- Note what students asked. Their questions are your next talk.

Read more about [alumni speaker programmes](/en/alumni-speaker-programmes) to see how different universities structure this.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a PhD or teaching experience to guest lecture at my old university?
No. Alumni pages ask for professional expertise and work experience, not academic credentials. Stockholm University, for example, asks alumni to present their expertise or work experience to students [[1]](#ref-1).

### Should I contact the alumni office or the course teacher?
Contact the teacher if your expertise fits a specific course. Contact the alumni office if you want a career talk or a panel, or if you do not know which course fits. Some universities, such as Stockholm University, accept both.

### Will I be paid for speaking at my alma mater?
Usually not. Most alumni pages do not mention a fee, and some say outright that they do not pay external participants [[3]](#ref-3). Ask about expenses before you accept.

### Can I speak online if I live abroad?
Sometimes. The University of Edinburgh Business School says its alumni speaker opportunities run both in person and online [[8]](#ref-8). Ask the alumni office whether remote sessions are possible.

### What if my old programme no longer exists?
Look for the closest current course in the same department. Departments often rename or merge programmes, and the alumni office can point you to the right place.
