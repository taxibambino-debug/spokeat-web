# Call for speakers: how to find open calls and write a strong submission

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

A call for speakers is the one time an organiser openly asks people like you to pitch. The door is open, the format is set and there is a deadline. That makes it a good place to start, but also a crowded one: everyone else can see the same call.

This guide covers where open calls are listed, how to judge a call before you spend an evening on it, and how to write a submission a review committee can say yes to. It complements the other route, pitching organisers who do not advertise, which is where Spoke At focuses most of its research.

## Key takeaways

- Open calls are listed on the event's own site, on submission platforms such as Sessionize and on community-run lists such as confs.tech.
- Check five things before you apply: audience fit, format, deadline and decision date, cost, and rules on sales content and recording.
- A strong submission names a specific audience, states clear takeaways and includes a short outline with timings.
- Product pitches are a common reason for rejection. Many calls exclude them outright.
- Submit early and keep a calendar of deadlines. Most talks never have an open call, so pair calls with direct pitching.

## What a call for speakers is

A call for speakers (CfS) or call for papers (CfP) is a public invitation to propose a session. Some events call it a "call for content" or "call for proposals". The basics are usually the same: a submission window, a form, a review by a programme committee or organisers, and a decision date.

The form normally asks for a title, an abstract or description, a level, a speaker bio and sometimes an outline. For example, DevSum 2027 in Stockholm asks for a short bio and a profile photo, runs all sessions in English, and asks speakers to label each session as introductory, intermediate or advanced [[1]](#ref-1).

## Where open calls are listed

| Where | What you find | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| The event's own website and social channels | The official call page, rules and deadline | Calls are often announced on social media first |
| Submission platforms (for example Sessionize) | Individual call pages; Sessionize also has a "Discover events" feature | Not every event uses a platform |
| Community lists (for example confs.tech) | Crowdsourced lists of conferences with call deadlines, filterable by topic | Coverage is strongest in tech; check every entry on the event's own site |
| Professional bodies and industry events | Calls for content from associations and trade events | Some calls charge selected speakers; read the terms |
| Meetup and user groups | Rolling calls with no fixed deadline | Small audiences, but good practice and fast decisions |

**Event websites.** Sessionize advises speakers to look on the event's official website for a link called "Call for Speakers", "Call for Papers", "Speak" or "Submit a talk", and to check social media next, since organisers often announce calls there [[2]](#ref-2). If there is no public call, Sessionize suggests contacting the organisers, who may invite you privately or tell you the line-up is closed [[2]](#ref-2).

**Platforms.** Sessionize says you can find events accepting submissions through its "Discover events" feature or by searching the web [[3]](#ref-3). AWS User Group Stockholm, for example, runs a rolling call on Sessionize that is open until November 2027 and welcomes talks "in any format or at any level of expertise" [[4]](#ref-4).

**Community lists.** confs.tech is an open-source, crowdsourced list of tech conferences. It covers more than 30 topics and shows upcoming calls for papers; organisers add events through the site or by pull request [[5]](#ref-5). Because anyone can add an entry, always confirm the deadline on the event's own page.

**Industry and professional events.** Outside tech, calls are often found on the event's own site under names like "call for content". Product management organiser Mind the Product, for example, takes talk ideas through an application form and suggests that new speakers start at a local ProductTank meetup [[6]](#ref-6). Our guide on [how to get speaking opportunities](/en/how-to-get-speaking-opportunities) covers the routes that have no open call at all.

## Keeping track of open calls: platforms, newsletters and alerts

Most calls close months before the event, so the hard part is noticing them in time. Four habits help.

**Check Sessionize regularly.** Sessionize describes itself as a tool for call for papers, scheduling and speaker management. Its Community plan is free for free community events, so it is worth checking for meetup and user-group calls as well as conferences [[12]](#ref-12). Each call has its own page with exact dates. DevSum 2027, for example, takes submissions from 8 October to 8 December 2026 for a conference in late May 2027 [[1]](#ref-1).

**Note last year's dates.** Last year's call is a useful guide to when the next one might open. Øredev's call for its November 2026 conference in Malmö ran from 1 to 31 March 2026 [[13]](#ref-13). If you missed a call, write down when it opened and set a reminder for the same month next year.

**Subscribe to event and association newsletters.** If an event you want to speak at has a newsletter, sign up. It is a simple way to hear when a call opens, and a newsletter archive can show what topics they cover.

**Use curated lists with care.** Some services collect calls for you. Innovation Women, for example, says it lists "hundreds of calls for speakers" for its members, with a focus on women and other under-represented groups [[14]](#ref-14). Whatever list you use, confirm the deadline and terms on the event's own call page before you write anything.

We also looked at PaperCall, another call-for-papers platform, but could not open its own pages when we checked, so we do not describe it here.

## How to judge a call before you apply

A good submission takes a few hours. Spend ten minutes first checking whether the call is worth it.

**1. Audience fit.** Read the tracks and past programmes. If the room is not your peers, buyers or future hires, skip it.

**2. Format and length.** Talks at Nordic Tech Days 2027 are 30 minutes, with workshops of two or four hours [[7]](#ref-7). DevSum 2027 talks are 50 minutes including questions [[1]](#ref-1). Make sure your material fits the slot.

**3. Deadline and decision date.** DevSum's call closes on 8 December 2026 [[1]](#ref-1). Money20/20 Europe's call for content was open from 2 to 27 November 2026 and the organisers aim to reply by the end of February 2027 [[8]](#ref-8). That is a three-month wait, so plan your other commitments around it.

**4. Cost and what is covered.** This varies more than most people expect:

| Event (as published) | What speakers get or pay |
|---|---|
| DevSum 2027, Stockholm | Free for speakers; travel and accommodation expenses covered [[1]](#ref-1) |
| Nordic Tech Days 2027, Tallinn | Free for speakers; travel up to €350 (€500 transatlantic) and two hotel nights [[7]](#ref-7) |
| Accelerate Tomorrow Summit 2027 | Free and selected on merit; two-day pass, but no fee, travel or hotel [[9]](#ref-9) |
| KuppingerCole Nordics Identity Impact Day 2027 | Complimentary conference registration for selected speakers [[10]](#ref-10) |
| Money20/20 Europe 2027 | Selected applicants are offered attendance at €2,495 + VAT; the suggesting company covers its speakers' costs [[8]](#ref-8) |

If a call charges speakers, read our honest take on [paying to speak and how to spot a bad deal](/en/paying-to-speak).

**5. Rules on sales and recording.** Nordic Tech Days does not accept sessions that promote a tool, product, service or consultancy, and says accepted talks may be recorded and published [[7]](#ref-7). AWS User Group Stockholm does not accept "sales speeches" [[4]](#ref-4). KuppingerCole asks for final slides, which are made available to attendees for download [[10]](#ref-10). Know what you are agreeing to before you submit.

## How to write a strong submission

Review committees read many proposals in a short time. Make yours easy to say yes to.

**Read the call twice.** Match your session to a named track or theme. Sessionize advises reading the description to see which topics and formats the organisers want, and checking dates, location and deadline before you start [[3]](#ref-3).

**Name a specific audience.** PyCon's long-standing proposal advice asks who the talk is for and says to be specific: "Python programmers" is not a good answer [[11]](#ref-11). "Payments product managers moving to instant payments" is a better kind of answer than "people in fintech".

**State clear takeaways.** What will people know or be able to do afterwards that they could not before? PyCon's advice puts this at the centre of the abstract [[11]](#ref-11).

**Add an outline with timings.** PyCon asks for an enumerated list of what you will cover with a time estimate for each part, and notes that incomplete outlines and bios can get a proposal cut in the first round [[11]](#ref-11). You do not need finished slides, but you should know your main points and roughly how long each takes.

**Do not pitch a product.** PyCon describes "how to use our product" talks as usually not appropriate, while talks about how you or your company solved a problem are welcome [[11]](#ref-11). The Accelerate Tomorrow Summit says its sessions focus on "experience and insight, not sales pitches" and directs companies wanting to showcase products to its partnership route [[9]](#ref-9).

**Show you can deliver.** Link to earlier talks, slides, video or articles if you have them [[11]](#ref-11). Mind the Product asks for a video if you have spoken before [[6]](#ref-6). If you have not, a meetup talk is the quickest way to build that evidence.

**Write a bio for this event.** Sessionize suggests explaining in your bio why you suit the event [[2]](#ref-2).

**Here is a hypothetical example** of the difference a specific abstract makes. Weak: "An overview of AI in banking and what it means for the future." Stronger: "For compliance leads at mid-sized banks: three lessons from moving transaction monitoring alerts to a machine-learning triage model, what broke in the first 90 days and the checks we added." The second tells reviewers who it is for and what they will get.

**Submit early.** PyCon advises submitting early because reviewers can give feedback before the deadline [[11]](#ref-11). Sessionize suggests putting the closing date in your calendar [[2]](#ref-2).

**Do not flood the call.** DevSum allows up to five proposals per speaker [[1]](#ref-1), and Sessionize notes that some events set limits [[2]](#ref-2). Two or three well-aimed sessions are usually better than five variations.

## After you submit

Use an email address you check often, because organisers will use it to contact you [[3]](#ref-3). On Sessionize you can track submissions from your speaker dashboard and reuse a session from your profile for another event [[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3).

Rejections are part of the process, and a no from one committee says little about the next. Ask for feedback if the organisers offer it, adjust and submit elsewhere.

Most guest lectures and society talks never have an open call, so keep pitching in parallel.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a call for speakers and a call for papers?
They are often used for the same thing: a public invitation to propose a session. In academia, a call for papers usually means submitting a written paper for peer review. At industry and tech events, both terms usually mean a talk or workshop proposal.

### When do calls for speakers open?
It depends on the event. DevSum's call for its late-May 2027 conference closes on 8 December 2026, and the Nordic Tech Days call for its June 2027 event closes on 31 December 2026, so both close about five to six months ahead. Meetup groups often run rolling calls with no fixed deadline.

### Do conferences pay speakers?
Some cover travel and accommodation, some give a free ticket, and some charge selected speakers. Read the call's terms before you submit, and see our guide to paying to speak if a fee is involved.

### Can I submit the same talk to several events?
Usually yes, unless the call asks for original or unpublished content. Adapt the title and abstract to each audience, and tell organisers if a recording of the talk already exists.
