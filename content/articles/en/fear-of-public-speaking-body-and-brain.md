# Fear of public speaking: what happens in your body and brain

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)


Your heart pounds. Your mouth goes dry. Your mind goes blank on the sentence you rehearsed ten times. If that sounds familiar, you are in large company: fear of public speaking is one of the most common social fears that researchers measure.

Below, based on peer-reviewed research and public-health sources: what happens in your body and brain, what the research cannot tell you, and when to talk to a doctor or psychologist.

This is general information. It is not a diagnosis and not treatment advice.

## Key takeaways
- Fear of public speaking is common. A Swedish population survey found it was the most common social fear, and studies report that 21% to 33% of people in community samples experience it.
- Speaking in front of people who judge you is a specific kind of stress. Lab research shows that tasks with social evaluation produce some of the largest stress-hormone responses.
- The physical signs (racing heart, sweating, shaking) are part of a normal stress response. They are unpleasant, but they are not dangerous in themselves.
- Speakers tend to overestimate how nervous they look.
- If the fear stops you from doing things you want or need to do, talk to a GP or other healthcare provider.

## How common is the fear of public speaking?

Very common. In a Swedish postal survey of 1,202 adults, public speaking was "the most common social fear" [[1]](#ref-1). A 2019 meta-analysis summarises earlier community studies as reporting prevalence rates for public speaking fear "ranging from 21 … to 33%" [[2]](#ref-2).

Two limits matter here. First, these figures come from surveys that define "fear" in different ways, so they vary a lot. Second, feeling nervous before a talk is not the same as having an anxiety disorder. Many people who dislike speaking in public do not have a clinical condition.

## What happens in your body

### The stress response starts before you speak

When your brain judges a situation as a threat, it activates the body's stress systems. One of these is the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. A major review describes the chain: the hypothalamus releases a hormone (CRH), which signals the pituitary gland to release ACTH, which in turn "triggers the adrenal cortex to release cortisol into the bloodstream" [[3]](#ref-3).

The same review found that cortisol typically peaks 21 to 40 minutes after a psychological stressor begins [[3]](#ref-3). That helps explain why some people feel worst in the waiting period before their slot, and why the feeling can linger after the talk.

### The signs you notice

What you notice first are the physical signs. The US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) lists physical signs that people with social anxiety can experience in performance situations, including that they "blush, sweat, or tremble", "have a rapid heart rate", or feel their "mind going blank" [[4]](#ref-4).

Many people without any anxiety disorder notice milder versions of the same signs before a talk. The difference is usually in intensity, how long it lasts, and how much it affects your life.

### Why speaking is such a strong trigger

Not all stress is equal. A meta-analysis of 208 laboratory studies by Dickerson and Kemeny found that psychological stressors did not raise cortisol equally. Tasks that were uncontrollable or involved "social-evaluative threat" (the risk of being judged by others) produced responses, and tasks combining both "were associated with the largest cortisol" changes and "the longest times to recovery" [[3]](#ref-3).

Public speaking fits: you are visible, you are being evaluated, and you cannot control how the audience reacts.

**Limits of this research:** these are lab studies, often with students, using standard tasks. They measure average responses across groups. They do not predict exactly how any one person will react on a real stage.

## What happens in your brain

### Self-focus adds fuel

Being judged by others is one trigger. Judging yourself may be another. In an experiment with 120 participants who gave a speech, Denson and colleagues compared three conditions: speaking to an evaluative panel, speaking while watching yourself live on a screen (self-focus), and a neutral control. Among men, cortisol "increased comparably" in the panel and self-focus conditions, but not in the control [[5]](#ref-5).

The authors suggest negative evaluation may raise cortisol whether it comes from others or from yourself [[5]](#ref-5). Limits: the effect appeared in men only, not in women, and it is a single study.

### You feel more visible than you are

Anxious speakers often believe their nerves are obvious. Research on the "illusion of transparency" suggests otherwise. A peer-reviewed replication summarises Savitsky and Gilovich's 2003 findings: speakers overestimated how visible their nervousness was, and the effects of this illusion were reduced by "simply assuring speakers" that their anxiety was less visible than they believed [[6]](#ref-6).

A related finding comes from a doctoral study at the University of Maryland: in a small sample of 78 people, shy participants (with and without social phobia) rated their own speech performance lower than independent observers did [[7]](#ref-7). In the same study, the groups did not differ on heart rate or skin conductance [[7]](#ref-7). It is one small dissertation, not a peer-reviewed journal article, so treat it as a pointer.

### What brain scans show, and their limits

In a brain-imaging (PET) study at Uppsala University, 18 people with social phobia and 6 comparison participants gave short speeches, once alone and once to a small silent audience [[10]](#ref-10). Speaking in public increased blood flow in the amygdala, a region involved in detecting threat, more in the social phobia group. In several areas of the cortex, blood flow fell in that group but rose in the comparison group [[10]](#ref-10). The authors linked social phobia to an evolutionarily older danger-recognition system, and the comparison pattern to cortical evaluation.

A 2007 meta-analysis by Etkin and Wager pooled imaging studies of emotional processing. People with social anxiety disorder, like those with specific phobia or PTSD, showed more activity in the amygdala and insula than comparison groups [[11]](#ref-11).

**Limits:** the Uppsala study was small, its comparison group was younger, and its statistics were not corrected for multiple comparisons [[10]](#ref-10). The meta-analysis included only eight social anxiety studies, and some shared participants [[11]](#ref-11). Both describe group averages in people with a diagnosis. They cannot show what happens in your own brain, and they say little about ordinary nerves.

### Threat, prediction and avoidance

Your brain tries to protect you from social harm. When it predicts embarrassment, it pushes you to escape or avoid. Sweden's public health portal 1177 notes that the more you try to avoid what you fear, the stronger the anxiety tends to become [[8]](#ref-8).

## Is this normal nervousness or something more?

Most speaking nerves are a normal response to a demanding situation. For some people, the fear is strong, persistent, and limits their work, studies or social life.

The NHS describes social anxiety disorder as "a long-term and overwhelming fear of social situations", and says it is a good idea to see a GP if you think you have it [[9]](#ref-9). NIMH notes that for some people the anxiety "may arise only during performance situations such as giving a speech" [[4]](#ref-4).

Only a qualified professional can assess whether what you experience is a clinical condition. If you are unsure, read our guide on [when the fear of speaking needs professional help](/en/when-fear-of-speaking-needs-help).

## What the research does not say

The research above does **not** show that:

- every speaker has the same stress response (the studies report group averages);
- cortisol or heart rate tells you how well you will speak;
- one technique works for everyone.

Some popular claims are contested. For example, the idea that standing in a "power pose" changes your hormones did not hold up in a larger replication. We cover that and other evidence in [how to calm nerves before a talk](/en/calm-nerves-before-a-talk).

## When to seek professional help

Talk to your GP or another healthcare provider if:

- the fear is persistent or severe;
- you avoid opportunities at work or in your studies because of it;
- you experience panic attacks;
- you use alcohol or other substances to cope with social situations.

In the UK, you can also refer yourself directly to an NHS talking therapies service without a GP referral [[9]](#ref-9). In the US, NIMH suggests talking to a health care provider such as a primary care doctor, psychiatrist, psychologist or clinical social worker [[4]](#ref-4).

If you or someone you know is in immediate danger or thinking about suicide, call your local emergency number now. In the US, NIMH lists the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) [[4]](#ref-4).

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my heart race before I speak in public?
Speaking in front of others is a situation where you can be judged, which activates the body's stress response. Research shows that tasks involving social evaluation trigger some of the largest stress-hormone responses in the lab. A racing heart is a common, normal part of that response.

### Is the fear of public speaking a mental illness?
Not in itself. Feeling nervous before speaking is very common. When the fear is intense, lasting, and limits your life, it can be part of social anxiety disorder, which a doctor or psychologist can assess.

### Can the audience see how nervous I am?
Usually less than you think. Research on the "illusion of transparency" found that speakers overestimate how visible their nervousness is to others.

### Does the fear of public speaking go away?
It can be reduced. A 2019 meta-analysis of 30 randomised controlled trials found that psychological interventions reduced the fear of public speaking. If your fear does not improve, or gets worse, talk to a healthcare provider.

## Start small: find a low-stakes first talk

Many people find that the first talk is the hardest. A short slot for a friendly audience, such as a student society or a local meetup, can be a gentler starting point than a conference stage.
