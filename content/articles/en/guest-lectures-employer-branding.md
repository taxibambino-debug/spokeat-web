# Guest lectures as employer branding: attract talent by teaching

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

If you run a team or a company, you know the problem. The graduates you want to hire have never heard of you. Large employers fill career fairs and sponsor student events. A 40-person fintech or a regional engineering firm cannot compete on budget.

A guest lecture is a cheaper and more credible way in. One of your engineers, analysts or product people teaches a session in a course your future hires are taking. Students see what the work is really like and who does it. They remember the company behind the person.

This guide is for managers, founders and HR leads. It explains why lectures work for employer branding, where the line between teaching and selling goes, who should speak and how to turn lectures into a recruiting pipeline.

## Key takeaways
- Employer image affects recruitment outcomes, and it is shaped by contact with recruiters and by word of mouth [[1]](#ref-1). A guest lecture creates both.
- Universities openly present guest lectures as a way for companies to meet future employees [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3).
- Teach in courses, recruit at company events. Some schools ask guests to focus on course content rather than company presentations [[4]](#ref-4).
- Send the people who do the work. Students value speakers who currently work in the field and talk about their daily work [[5]](#ref-5).
- Plan a next step after the lecture: a society event, a thesis project or an internship.

## What employer branding is, and why lectures fit

Employer branding is how an organisation shapes the way potential employees see it as a place to work. A major review of the research by Filip Lievens and Jerel Slaughter, published in the *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* in 2016, covers studies of employer image since 2001. It describes the benefits of a favourable employer image, including recruitment outcomes, and discusses what shapes that image, such as recruiters and word of mouth [[1]](#ref-1).

A guest lecture fits that picture well. The speaker acts as an informal recruiter, but one who is there to teach. Students who found the session useful talk about it with classmates. And the company is seen through the work it does, not through an advertisement.

The research on employer branding does not test guest lectures as such. So treat this as a reasoned fit with what the research says about how image is formed, not as proof that a lecture will raise your applications.

## Universities say it themselves

You do not need to sneak employer branding into a lecture. Universities describe the link openly.

- **Lund University School of Economics and Management (LUSEM)** says guest lectures help partner companies "build long-term connections with future leaders and the academic community". It separately offers company presentations, where organisations introduce their culture and career opportunities [[2]](#ref-2).
- **KTH** tells companies that a guest speaker also represents their industry, and that companies can host study visits to promote themselves to possible future employees [[3]](#ref-3).
- **Jönköping International Business School (JIBS)** lists strengthening your brand as one benefit of hosting students or sending a guest lecturer [[6]](#ref-6).
- **The University of Edinburgh Business School** describes its alumni career lounges as a way to share your career journey and introduce potential future talent to your business and industry [[7]](#ref-7).

This matters because it means teachers and career services expect companies to have an interest. You can be honest about it, as long as the session gives students real value.

## Teaching versus selling: pick the right format

The biggest mistake is turning a course lecture into a company pitch. Aalto University School of Business asks guest lecturers to focus on course content rather than a company presentation [[4]](#ref-4). Teachers who feel their session was used for advertising rarely invite the company back.

The solution is to use different formats for different goals:

| Format | Main goal | Company content | Where to arrange it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guest lecture in a course | Teach a topic, show competence | Minimal: examples from your work | Course coordinator |
| Student society lunch or breakfast lecture | Show the company and its jobs | Expected | Society's corporate relations team |
| Company presentation or roundtable | Meet students interested in your industry | Expected | Career services |
| Study visit | Show the workplace | Expected | Teacher or career services |
| Thesis project or case | Work with students on a real problem | Built in | Teacher or career services |

Student societies are open about the company angle. The Corporate Relations Committee of LundaEkonomerna, the student union at LUSEM, runs one-hour lunch lectures in which companies explain how they operate, what opportunities they offer students and why they are a good place to work [[8]](#ref-8). That is the right place for your recruiting message. Read more in [how to speak at student societies](/en/speak-at-student-societies).

In a course lecture, your brand comes through competence. Students see a skilled practitioner from your company explaining a hard problem well. That is often more persuasive than any slide about your benefits.

## Who should speak

Send the people students want to become. In a survey of 114 undergraduates about industry guest speakers, students valued guests who currently worked in a field related to the course, spoke with enthusiasm and candour, and answered questions. They especially liked hearing about the speaker's day-to-day work life and getting career advice [[5]](#ref-5). The study is small and from one subject area, but the message is clear.

In practice:

- **Choose practitioners over HR.** A data engineer explaining a real pipeline is more credible than a recruiter describing the culture.
- **Include recent graduates.** A speaker two or three years out of university is someone students can picture themselves becoming.
- **Prepare them, but do not script them.** Give speakers time to prepare, agree on what can be shared publicly, and let them speak in their own words.
- **Support first-time speakers.** A panel or Q&A format is a gentler start than a solo lecture.

## How lectures feed recruiting

A lecture on its own is a single touchpoint. It works better as the first step of a sequence. Universities already provide most of the steps:

1. **Guest lecture in a course.** Students meet your people and your field.
2. **A follow-up event.** A student society lunch lecture or a career services roundtable for those who want to know more [[2]](#ref-2) [[8]](#ref-8).
3. **A thesis project, case or internship.** LUSEM notes that some programmes let students write their thesis with a company or organisation [[9]](#ref-9).
4. **A job advert where students look.** Lund University's job portal CareerHub is free for employers advertising only to Lund University students [[9]](#ref-9). Uppsala University points alumni who want to recruit to Studentkraft and UU CareerGate [[10]](#ref-10).

At the end of a course lecture, ask the teacher whether you may mention one next step in a single sentence, such as an internship programme or a thesis topic. Many will say yes if the session itself was useful.

## Building a lecture programme

One lecture is a test. A programme is a strategy.

- **Pick three to five courses** in the fields you hire from. Read our guide on [how to become a guest lecturer at a university](/en/become-a-guest-lecturer-at-a-university) to find them.
- **Aim for recurring slots.** A teacher who liked your session will often want it again next year. That builds a presence over several cohorts.
- **Spread the load.** Rotate speakers so no one person carries the whole programme.
- **Combine with society events.** A course lecture in the autumn and a society lunch lecture a few weeks later reach the same students twice.

There is also a reputational benefit beyond hiring. Teaching shows expertise to peers, partners and customers. Our article on [how speaking builds industry authority](/en/speaking-builds-industry-authority) covers that side.

## How to measure it

Be honest about what you can measure. You will not get a clean return-on-investment figure from a lecture. You can track signals:

- Add "Where did you hear about us?" to internship and graduate applications, with the universities you visit as options.
- Count applications from the universities and programmes where you lecture, year on year.
- Note invitations back. A teacher who invites you again is a sign the session worked.
- Ask speakers what students asked afterwards. Questions about jobs are a good sign.

## A hypothetical example

A founder of a 40-person payments company finds that few business students in her city know the company. She asks two senior engineers and one product manager to offer guest lectures. Each picks a course: one in payment systems, one in software engineering, one in product management. They teach real problems from their work and keep company content to a single slide. A month later, the company hosts a lunch lecture at the business students' society, where it talks openly about internships. The next spring, the company adds the three universities to its application form question and starts tracking the answers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it acceptable to recruit during a guest lecture?
Keep the course lecture about the subject. Some schools say so explicitly [[4]](#ref-4). Ask the teacher if you may mention one next step, and do your recruiting at a society event, roundtable or career fair.

### Do companies pay to give guest lectures?
None of the university guest lecture pages we checked mention a fee for giving a course lecture. Some company events run through student societies or career services may be part of partnerships, so ask how they work before you commit.

### Who in the company should give the lecture?
A practitioner who does the work the students are studying. Students value speakers who currently work in a related field and talk about their daily work [[5]](#ref-5).

### How soon will we see more applications?
Do not expect a quick effect. One lecture is a single contact with one cohort. Plan for recurring lectures over several terms and track the signals above.
