# Emotions on stage: how speakers connect with an audience

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

Most speakers can tell when a room is with them and when it is not. Heads come up, phones go down, people laugh at the right moments and ask sharp questions afterwards. When it does not happen, the talk feels like reading aloud to a wall.

"Connect with your audience" is easy to say and hard to do. This article breaks it into parts that research can actually speak to: attention, emotion and understanding. It covers what the classical rhetorical tradition says, what modern studies support, where popular claims go further than the evidence, and what you can do in your next guest lecture or panel.

## Key takeaways

- Aristotle treated emotion as part of how an audience judges, not as a trick to get around judgement [[1]](#ref-1)[[2]](#ref-2).
- Research on "transportation" links absorption in a talk or story to focused attention, mental imagery and feeling. Distraction reduces it [[4]](#ref-4)[[5]](#ref-5).
- Emotional appeals do change attitudes and behaviour, but often by a small amount. They work better when people are shown what they can do [[3]](#ref-3).
- Brain studies suggest that listeners who understand a speaker track and even anticipate them. Popular claims about mirror neurons go further than the evidence [[6]](#ref-6)[[7]](#ref-7).
- Connection is built from relevance, honest feeling and a clear point, not from performance tricks.

## What "connecting" means

Aristotle described one of the three proofs a speech can offer as "putting the hearer into a certain frame of mind". He wrote that the orator persuades through the hearers "when they are roused to emotion by his speech" [[1]](#ref-1).

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy explains why this matters. For Aristotle, emotions change how people judge. He analysed specific emotions, such as anger, so that a speaker could understand the conditions under which an audience feels them [[2]](#ref-2).

That gives a useful working definition. Connecting with an audience means that:

1. they pay attention,
2. they feel that the topic matters to them, and
3. they understand and can follow you.

Each part has research behind it, with different strengths.

## Attention: absorption and its enemies

The most relevant research comes from studies of "narrative transportation". Green and Brock described transportation as absorption that combines attention, mental imagery and feeling. People who were more absorbed in a story held beliefs closer to the story's message and argued against it less [[4]](#ref-4).

A 2024 review by Green and Appel adds detail that matters for speakers [[5]](#ref-5):

- **Attention rises and falls.** Transportation is dynamic, not a switch that stays on.
- **Distraction cuts it.** Secondary tasks and distractions reduce transportation.
- **People differ.** Some people have a stronger general tendency to get absorbed than others.
- **Expectations matter.** Positive reviews increased transportation, possibly by raising expectations.

These studies mostly use written or filmed stories, not live talks. The direction of the findings is still a reasonable guide: anything that splits attention makes it harder for your audience to feel and follow what you say.

## Emotion: what it does and does not do

### Emotion can support memory

In their meta-analysis of stories and memory, Mar and colleagues list emotion as one possible reason stories are remembered better: stories evoke emotion more readily, and emotional content tends to be better remembered [[8]](#ref-8). They present this as an explanation drawn from earlier research, not something their analysis tested directly.

### Emotion can shift attitudes, modestly

Fear is the most studied emotional appeal. A meta-analysis of 127 articles and more than 27,000 participants found that fear appeals had a positive but small effect on attitudes, intentions and behaviour. They worked better when the message included efficacy statements, telling people what they could do [[3]](#ref-3). The lead author stressed that fear appeals are not a cure-all because the effect is small [[3]](#ref-3).

The lesson for speakers: emotion without a way forward is weaker than emotion with one. If you raise a concern, show the room what to do about it.

### Emotion does not replace substance

Aristotle's aim was a sound judgement by the audience [[2]](#ref-2). An emotional peak with nothing behind it may be remembered as a moment, but not as an argument. See [ethos, pathos and logos for professionals](/en/ethos-pathos-logos) for how emotion fits with credibility and reasoning.

## Understanding: what brain studies suggest

In a study by Stephens, Silbert and Hasson, a speaker told an unrehearsed, real-life story while her brain was scanned. Listeners then heard the recording in the scanner. Brain activity in speaker and listeners was coupled across many regions, and the coupling vanished when listeners did not understand the language. Stronger coupling went with better comprehension. Some listener regions were active before the speaker's, which the authors interpret as prediction [[6]](#ref-6).

Two cautions apply. First, the study was small, with one English-speaking storyteller and 11 to 12 listeners, and the main link to comprehension was only marginally significant [[6]](#ref-6). Second, it is tempting to explain "connection" through mirror neurons. A review by Dinstein and colleagues concluded that human studies had mostly not shown the movement-selective responses found in monkeys, and that broad brain activity during observation cannot be used to test theories about human mirror neurons [[7]](#ref-7).

The safer conclusion: understanding a speaker involves following and anticipating them. Clear structure makes that easier.

## Practical ways to connect

The suggestions below follow from the research above. They are practical guidance, not tested interventions.

### Before the talk
- **Find the stake for this audience.** Aristotle's approach was to understand the conditions for an emotion [[2]](#ref-2). Ask what this group worries about, wants or will face next. For example, a hypothetical product manager speaking to a student society might focus on the first six months in a junior role, not on company strategy.
- **Choose one feeling and one point.** Curiosity, concern or confidence. Pick one and tie it to the main message.
- **Plan an action.** If your talk raises a concern, prepare a concrete step the audience can take [[3]](#ref-3).

### During the talk
- **Remove competition for attention.** Distraction reduces absorption [[5]](#ref-5), so do not talk over dense slides. Blank the screen for key moments.
- **Make it easy to anticipate.** Listeners who understand a speaker appear to predict what comes next [[6]](#ref-6). Help them: say where you are going, then go there.
- **Use a specific case.** Absorption involves mental imagery and feeling [[4]](#ref-4), and a concrete situation gives the audience something to picture. See [the science of storytelling](/en/science-of-storytelling-in-talks).
- **Let your own response show, honestly.** If something frustrated or surprised you, say so. Do not fake emotion; it risks your credibility.

### Reading the room
Watch for signs of attention, such as people looking up, nodding or taking notes. If attention drops, change something: ask a question, move to an example, or pause. Treat these as cues, not proof. Body language is easy to over-read; see [what the research says about voice and body language](/en/voice-and-body-language-research).

| Goal | What to try | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | One idea per slide, pauses, a clear outline | Talking over busy slides |
| Relevance | Name the audience's next step or problem | Generic industry trends |
| Feeling | One honest, specific case | Manufactured drama |
| Action | A concrete step people can take | Raising alarm with no way forward |

## Where the evidence is limited

- Most studies of absorption and emotional appeals use reading, video or online samples, not live talks [[5]](#ref-5)[[8]](#ref-8).
- Effects of emotional appeals are often small [[3]](#ref-3).
- Neuroscience of speaker and listener is promising but based on small samples, and some popular explanations, such as mirror neurons, are contested [[6]](#ref-6)[[7]](#ref-7).

Use the research to sharpen your judgement, not to replace it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I connect with an audience I do not know?
Find out who will be in the room and what they are dealing with. Ask the organiser or course coordinator what the group cares about. Build the talk around one problem they recognise and one step they can take.

### Should I show emotion when I speak?
Showing honest reactions to your own material usually helps the audience understand why it matters. Faked emotion is risky because it can undermine trust. Aristotle treated emotion as part of good judgement, which means it should fit the facts.

### Do emotional stories always work better than facts?
No. Research suggests stories and statistics influence different outcomes, and emotional appeals often have small effects. A combination of a clear fact and a relevant, specific case is usually stronger than either alone.

### How do I keep attention in a long lecture?
Plan changes of pace, such as a question, an example or a short pause, and remove distractions like dense slides. Research on absorption shows that distraction reduces it, so protect key moments. Make your structure clear so people can anticipate where you are going.

### Is audience connection about mirror neurons?
That is a popular explanation, but the evidence for mirror neurons in humans is weaker than their fame suggests. Brain studies do show that listeners who understand a speaker track and anticipate them. Clear structure and relevance are better bets than neuroscience-inspired tricks.
