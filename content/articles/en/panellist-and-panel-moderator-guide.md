# How to be a good panellist, and how to moderate a panel

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

Panels are everywhere: at conferences, association breakfasts, university alumni evenings and political forums. For many professionals a panel is the first time they speak in front of an audience in their professional role.

Panels are also easy to do badly. Panellists give mini-speeches, one person dominates, and the audience questions get squeezed into the last two minutes. This guide covers both sides: how to get invited and be a useful panellist, and how to moderate a panel that the audience actually enjoys.

## Key takeaways

- A panel is a good first slot: you share the stage, the moderator carries the structure, and you need less preparation than for a solo talk.
- To get invited, make yourself easy to find and tell organisers which topics you can cover. Some organisations openly offer panellists to event organisers.
- As a panellist, prepare two or three points, keep answers short and build on what others say.
- As a moderator, talk to the panellists beforehand, keep introductions to about 30 seconds and protect time for audience questions.
- Check the gender balance and range of voices. In one large study of medical conferences, 36.6% of panel sessions were all-male [[5]](#ref-5).

## Why panels are a good first slot

A panel lowers the barrier. You speak for a few minutes at a time, a moderator asks the questions and other people share the load. You still get the visibility of being on the programme.

Panels are common and varied. Uppsala University's development office, for example, ran a 90-minute panel conversation in October 2026 with a science journalist and three professors, led by a moderator [[8]](#ref-8). At Almedalen Week in Visby, where the 2024 programme had 2,124 official events [[6]](#ref-6), organisations both look for panellists and offer their own experts for panels and seminars [[7]](#ref-7).

The trade-off is that you control less. You cannot plan a full argument, and you depend on the moderator and the other panellists. That is why preparation looks different from preparing a talk.

## How to get invited onto a panel

Organisers usually build panels from people they already know or can easily find. Help them:

- **Be findable.** Keep a short public profile that states the two or three topics you can speak on.
- **Tell organisers you are available.** Associations, chambers and networks run many panels. Our guide on [speaking at industry associations and chambers](/en/speak-at-industry-associations-and-chambers) explains who decides there.
- **Offer a perspective the panel lacks.** Organisers want different views. A practitioner, a regulator and a researcher make a better panel than three people who agree.
- **Start at smaller events.** Meetups often run short panels and have fewer applicants. See our guide to [getting a first meetup slot](/en/meetup-talks-first-slot).

Some organisations make this explicit. Before Almedalen Week 2026, EY Sweden published a page telling organisers that its specialists were available for panels and seminars, with contact details for each spokesperson [[7]](#ref-7). If you work for a larger organisation, check whether your communications team does the same. For how Almedalen seminars work, see our guide to [speaking at Almedalen](/en/speaking-at-almedalen).

## Preparing as a panellist

**Ask for the briefing call.** A guide for moderators from the University of Toronto's Faculty of Medicine recommends a joint discussion with all panellists beforehand, where feasible, to agree what will be covered and how the session will flow [[1]](#ref-1). If the moderator does not suggest one, ask. Find out:

- how long the session is and how many panellists there are;
- the opening question and whether you introduce yourself;
- how audience questions will be handled;
- who else is on the panel and what they are likely to say.

**Choose two or three points.** You will not get to say everything. Decide which points you want the audience to remember, and prepare a short, concrete example for each.

**Keep your introduction short.** The Toronto guide suggests that each speaker introduces themselves in 30 seconds rather than reading long lists of qualifications [[1]](#ref-1).

**Know the other panellists.** Read their recent posts or talks. You will find it easier to agree, disagree or add something.

## On stage: being a good panellist

- **Answer the question, briefly.** A panel is a conversation. A moderator writing for Toastmasters International notes that moderators may need to interrupt panellists who turn answers into speeches [[2]](#ref-2). Aim for answers you can give in about a minute, then stop.
- **Build on others.** Refer to what the previous speaker said: agree and add, or disagree with a reason. That is what makes a panel better than three short talks.
- **Disagree politely and specifically.** A clear, respectful difference of view is useful to the audience.
- **Do not sell.** The audience came for the discussion, not a pitch.
- **Let others in.** If you have had more airtime than the others, say so and pass the word.
- **Use plain language.** The Swedish organisation Vetenskap & Allmänhet advises participants in its science cafés to use everyday language, avoid jargon and answer without long digressions [[9]](#ref-9).

Reading the room matters too. Our guide on [emotions on stage and connecting with an audience](/en/emotions-on-stage-connect-with-audience) covers what research says about that.

## How to moderate a panel

The moderator decides whether a panel works. These are practical steps drawn from published guidance.

### Before the event

- **Research the topic and the panellists**, and prepare questions that create real discussion [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Prepare more questions than you need.** The Toastmasters article suggests up to 10 questions, with at least one for each panellist, and a broad first question to warm up the panel and the audience [[2]](#ref-2).
- **Call each panellist.** Vetenskap & Allmänhet's tips for science café moderators say to call all participants for a quick chat before the event [[3]](#ref-3).
- **Agree the structure** in a joint call if possible [[1]](#ref-1).

### During the panel

- **Keep introductions to about 30 seconds each** [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Give everyone fair time.** The Toronto guide says to ensure all speakers get equal time and an equal number of questions, and to interrupt respectfully and bring in others if one person talks too much [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Keep questions short and simple**, so you can rephrase them naturally instead of reading them out [[2]](#ref-2).
- **Watch the clock.** Do not let speakers go beyond their time [[1]](#ref-1).

### Audience questions

- **Protect the time.** The Toronto guide recommends keeping at least 25% of the session for audience questions [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Have a question ready** in case the audience is slow to start [[3]](#ref-3).
- **Collect questions in other ways** too, such as cards or a polling tool [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Spread the questions.** Guidance on facilitating panel sessions hosted by the University of Washington's ADVANCE programme suggests actively inviting different voices and different parts of the room [[4]](#ref-4).

### Closing

Summarise the key points, thank the audience and then the panellists [[1]](#ref-1), and finish on time.

## Inclusive panels

Who sits on the panel shapes the discussion. A study in JAMA Network Open analysed 98 academic medical conferences from 2017 to 2018 across five regions. Women made up 30.1% of speakers, and 36.6% of panel sessions were all-male. The share of women on planning committees correlated with the share of female speakers [[5]](#ref-5). The authors note limits: they sampled one conference per region and specialty for a single year, and did not study why the gaps exist [[5]](#ref-5). The findings come from medicine and may not transfer directly to other fields.

As a moderator or organiser, you can check the line-up early and ask whom else to invite. As a panellist, you can ask who else is on the panel before you accept.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I get invited to speak on a panel?
Make it easy for organisers to find you and to see your topics. Tell associations, networks and event organisers in your field that you are available, and offer a perspective their panels lack. Smaller events such as meetups are a good place to start.

### How long should a panellist's answers be?
Short. A panel is a conversation, and long answers crowd out other panellists and the audience. Aim for about a minute, make one point with an example, then stop.

### How much time should a panel leave for audience questions?
A guide from the University of Toronto's Faculty of Medicine recommends reserving at least 25% of the session for audience questions. Have a question ready in case the audience is slow to start.

### What should a moderator do before a panel?
Research the topic, prepare more questions than you need, and talk to each panellist beforehand. A joint call with all panellists, where possible, helps agree the structure and avoid overlap.
