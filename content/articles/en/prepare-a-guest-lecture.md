# How to prepare a guest lecture students will remember

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

The teacher said yes. You have a date, a room or a video link, and a slot of perhaps 45 minutes. Now you have to fill it with something students will find useful.

It is tempting to prepare a guest lecture like a client presentation: slides about your company, a few achievements, and five minutes for questions at the end. Student surveys suggest that is close to what students like least.

This guide shows how to prepare a guest lecture that fits the course, keeps students involved and gives them something to take away. It draws on student surveys and research on active learning. It will not write your talk for you, and neither will Spoke At. But it will help you shape it.

## Key takeaways
- Start with a short call with the teacher. Agree how your session supports the course's learning objectives.
- Students say they value career insight, honest day-to-day stories and time for questions. They dislike speakers who talk about themselves too much.
- Build in interaction. Research on whole courses shows active learning improves results compared with lecturing alone.
- The "10-minute attention span" is a myth. The speaker matters more than the clock.
- Keep slides visual and light. Keep company background brief.

## Step 1: Brief yourself with the teacher

The teacher knows the course, the students and what they have already covered. A 20-minute call saves you hours. Aalto University School of Business says guests should agree the content of the lecture in advance with the responsible teacher, so it meets the course's learning objectives. It also asks guests to describe their company only briefly and to avoid a general company presentation [[1]](#ref-1).

Questions worth asking:

- **Learning objectives.** Which learning outcome should my session support?
- **Prior knowledge.** What have students covered so far? Which concepts can I build on?
- **Audience.** How many students, at what level, and from which programmes?
- **Format and length.** In person or online? How long is my slot, and how much time for questions?
- **Language.** Which language is the course taught in?
- **Assessment.** Will students use my material in an assignment? Will it be examined?
- **Logistics.** Room, technology, recording, and whether I should send slides in advance.

Formats vary a lot. Sheffield University Management School describes guest sessions of about 30 to 40 minutes plus 15 to 20 minutes for questions [[4]](#ref-4). RMIT Europe's online guest lectures range from a 15-minute Q&A, facilitated by the course coordinator, to a 30-minute presentation, live or pre-recorded [[9]](#ref-9).

On assessment, be aware of a tension. In one survey, 57.4% of students disagreed with being tested on guest content, although many said it motivated attendance [[2]](#ref-2). Follow the teacher's lead.

## Step 2: Know what students value

Two surveys of students give useful pointers. Both come from single US institutions in specific fields, so treat them as indications rather than rules.

In a survey of 107 strategic communication students, the most wanted topics were career advice (88.3%), networking tips (86.4%) and the speaker's professional journey (85.4%). A topic closely tied to the course was wanted by 72.5%. Students preferred visual aids (87.4%) and a conversational format (65.0%). About 71% rated Q&A as very or extremely important, and the most common recommendation was to save 11 to 20% of the talk for questions [[2]](#ref-2).

The same study lists what students disliked: speakers who talked too much about themselves, who talked "at" rather than "with" them, slides with too much text or read aloud, too little time for questions, and repetition of course material [[2]](#ref-2).

A second survey of 114 students found that guests were valued most when they currently worked in a field related to the course content, spoke enthusiastically and honestly, and answered questions. Students particularly valued insight into the speaker's day-to-day work and advice on their own career path [[3]](#ref-3).

Put together: link the course to real work, be honest about how things actually happen, and leave real time for questions.

## Step 3: Build a simple structure

This is a suggested structure for a 45-minute slot. It is our own template, not a research-based standard. Adjust it to the time and format you agreed with the teacher.

| Time | Part | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 min | Opening | Who you are in two sentences. Why this topic matters for this course. |
| 3–8 min | Question to the room | A short question or poll that connects to what students already know. |
| 8–25 min | Core content | Two or three points, each tied to a course concept and illustrated with one real case. |
| 25–32 min | Activity | Students discuss a short problem from your case in pairs or small groups. |
| 32–37 min | Debrief | Hear a few answers. Say what actually happened and why. |
| 37–45 min | Q&A | Open questions, including about careers. Roughly 15 to 20% of the slot. |

Three principles behind it:

1. **One case beats ten facts.** A real decision, with the constraints you faced, gives students something to analyse.
2. **Use the course's language.** If the course uses a framework, show where your case fits it. That links your talk to what they will be assessed on.
3. **Say what you do not know.** Honesty was one of the qualities students valued most [[3]](#ref-3).

## Step 4: Build in interaction

The strongest evidence comes from research on whole courses, not single guest sessions, but it points in a clear direction. A 2014 meta-analysis of 225 studies in undergraduate science, engineering and mathematics compared active learning with traditional lecturing. Exam and concept scores were 0.47 standard deviations higher with active learning, and average failure rates were 21.8% with active learning versus 33.8% with traditional lecturing [[5]](#ref-5). The studies were in STEM subjects, so the size of the effect may differ in other fields and in a one-off session.

Simple methods that work in a guest slot:

- **A quick poll or show of hands** at the start. Uppsala University lists clickers as one example of an activating element in lectures [[8]](#ref-8).
- **A short small-group task.** Uppsala describes giving students a task during a lecture, letting them discuss in small groups, and then taking the discussion to the whole class [[8]](#ref-8).
- **A "what would you do?" moment.** Stop your case at the point of decision and ask students to choose.
- **A recall question at the end.** Ask students to write down the one idea they will remember. Research on memory shows that actively retrieving information helps long-term retention more than re-reading it. In one set of experiments, repeated recall improved retention by more than 100% compared with dropping items from further testing [[7]](#ref-7). Those experiments used word lists in a lab, so treat this as a principle, not a precise prediction.

## A myth to drop: the 10-minute attention span

You may have heard that students stop paying attention after 10 to 15 minutes. A review in *Advances in Physiology Education* examined the evidence and found that the primary data do not support a 10 to 15 minute attention limit. It concluded that the greatest variability in student attention comes from differences between teachers [[6]](#ref-6).

So do not chop your talk into rigid 10-minute blocks out of fear. Use interaction because it helps learning, and focus on being clear, concrete and engaged.

## Step 5: Prepare slides and materials

- **Keep slides visual.** Students in the communication survey preferred visual aids but disliked text-heavy slides and speakers who read from them [[2]](#ref-2).
- **Keep company background brief.** One slide is usually enough. Aalto asks guests to avoid a general company presentation [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Send materials to the teacher in advance** if they want to share them with students.
- **Check confidentiality.** Remove anything your employer would not want on a course platform, since slides may be shared.
- **For online sessions,** test the platform with the teacher and plan how students will ask questions.

## Step 6: After the lecture

- **Thank the teacher** within a day or two.
- **Ask for one piece of feedback.** What worked, and what would they change?
- **Offer to return.** If it went well, ask whether you could contribute next time the course runs.
- **Share follow-up material** through the teacher, not directly to students, unless the teacher suggests otherwise.

A good first lecture is the best way to get a second one. A natural place to start is the university you know best. ## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a guest lecture be?
It depends on the course. Sheffield University Management School describes about 30 to 40 minutes plus 15 to 20 minutes of questions, while RMIT Europe's online format ranges from a 15-minute Q&A to a 30-minute talk [[4]](#ref-4) [[9]](#ref-9). Agree the length with the teacher.

### How much time should I leave for questions?
In one student survey, the most common recommendation was 11 to 20% of the talk [[2]](#ref-2). For a 45-minute slot, that means roughly 5 to 9 minutes. Agree the split with the teacher.

### Should I talk about my company?
Briefly. Give enough context for your examples to make sense, then move on. Aalto University School of Business asks guests to avoid a general company presentation [[1]](#ref-1).

### How do I make a guest lecture interactive?
Use a quick poll, a short small-group task, or a "what would you do?" question at a decision point in your case. Then hear a few answers and explain what really happened.

### What should I send the teacher before the lecture?
A short outline, the learning objective you are supporting, any slides they want to share, and your technical needs. Ask whether the session will be recorded.
