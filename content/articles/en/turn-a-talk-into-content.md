# Turn one talk into a month of content

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

You spent days preparing a 20-minute talk. Forty people heard it. Then it was over. That is a poor return on the work.

The research, structure and examples you built for one room can feed a month of posts, an article and a newsletter, and give you the recording and photos that organisers ask for when you pitch your next talk.

## Key takeaways
- Agree on recording, photos and slide sharing with the organiser before the event, not after.
- Capture four things on the day: a recording (even audio only), photos of you speaking, the audience's questions and your own notes on what landed.
- Publish one main piece per week for four weeks: a short post, a long article, a series of single-idea posts, then a newsletter.
- Consistency matters more than volume: in one survey, only 15% of decision-makers rated the expert content they read as very good or excellent.
- Photos and recordings of people can be personal data, and the photographer owns the rights to their photos. Ask before you publish.

## Why repurposing is worth the effort

There are two reasons.

**Consistency builds trust.** In the 2024 Edelman-LinkedIn survey of business decision-makers, 54% said organisations that consistently produce thought leadership had prompted them to research their offers [[1]](#ref-1). The survey is self-reported and sponsored by LinkedIn, so treat it as a signal, not proof. For individuals the logic is similar: one talk is an event, a month of follow-up is a body of work. Our article on [why speaking builds industry authority](/en/speaking-builds-industry-authority) goes into this in more detail.

**Good content is rare.** The same research found that only 15% of decision-makers rated the thought leadership they read as very good or excellent. The highest-rated content had strong research and data (55%) and concrete guidance and case studies (43%) [[1]](#ref-1). A well-prepared talk already has those ingredients, and the full report counts live presentations and webinars among thought-leadership formats [[4]](#ref-4).

There is also your next pitch. Mind the Product asks speakers who have spoken before to include a video of a past talk [[2]](#ref-2). Voxel51 asks for a profile photo, a short bio and a 4–5 sentence abstract [[3]](#ref-3). A talk you have captured properly gives you all of these.

## Before the talk: agree on the basics

Ask the organiser three questions when you confirm the slot.

1. **Will the talk be recorded, and can I use the recording?** If they record, ask whether you can share it or clips from it, and when. If they do not, ask whether you may record audio or video yourself.
2. **Will there be a photographer, and can I use the photos?** Under Swedish copyright law, the person who took a photo has the exclusive right to copy it and make it available to the public [[8]](#ref-8). Being in the picture does not give you that right, so ask for permission and credit the photographer. If there is no photographer, ask a colleague to take some photos of you on stage.
3. **May I share my slides afterwards?** Make sure your slides contain nothing confidential from your employer or clients. Also check every image you borrowed: a photo in your slides is still the photographer's to publish, not yours [[8]](#ref-8).

Credit the organiser in everything you publish. It costs nothing and makes them more likely to invite you back.

### Photos of the audience: a privacy check

Sweden's data protection authority, IMY, states that photos of people and digitally stored sound recordings can be personal data [[5]](#ref-5). That matters when attendees are recognisable.

- **Posting publicly is not "private use".** IMY says the GDPR does not apply to purely private use, but that sharing with a large number of people is not purely private. If you post so that anyone can see it, you need a legal basis. IMY adds that legitimate interest is rarely a suitable basis for private individuals sharing images of other private individuals [[7]](#ref-7).
- **Organisers have a route you lack.** IMY's guidance for organisations says collecting consent from everyone in an event photo is often impractical. A company can instead rely on a documented balancing of interests and should tell attendees that images may be used, that they can object and whom to contact [[6]](#ref-6). Another reason to use the organiser's official photos.
- **The simplest fix:** choose shots of you and your slides, and keep the audience out of frame or unrecognisable. For video on YouTube, the Studio editor has a face blur tool [[11]](#ref-11).

This is general information, not legal advice.

## On the day: capture four things

- **A recording.** A full video is ideal. A phone recording of the audio is enough to write from.
- **Photos of you speaking.** Two or three good ones are enough. You will use them for months on your [speaker profile](/en/speaker-profile-what-organisers-check).
- **The questions.** Write down every audience question straight after the talk, as close to word for word as you can. They show what people actually care about.
- **What landed.** Note which example, slide or phrase got a reaction. That is your opening line for the posts that follow.

## The four-week plan

Each week has one main piece.

| Week | Main piece | What it contains | Time needed (estimate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thank-you post | A photo, one key idea, a thank-you to the organiser | 30 minutes |
| 2 | Long article | The talk written out, plus the audience's questions answered | 2–4 hours |
| 3 | Single-idea posts | Three short posts, one idea each, with a clip or slide | 1–2 hours |
| 4 | Newsletter and profile update | A summary with links, and the talk added to your speaker record | 1 hour |

The time estimates are ours, based on a typical 20–30 minute talk. Yours will vary.

### Week 1: the thank-you post

Publish within a day or two while the event is fresh. Keep it short: one photo of you speaking, one sentence on where you spoke, the single idea you most want people to remember, and a thank-you to the organiser. Save the full summary for week 2.

### Week 2: the long article

Turn the talk into a written article of 1,000 to 1,500 words. This is the piece that lasts longest, because people can find it through search and you can link to it from your profile.

A simple structure:

1. The problem your talk addressed, in two or three sentences.
2. Your main points, one section each, with the examples you used on stage.
3. The audience's questions and your answers. This is often the most useful part.
4. A link to the recording or slides, if you have permission.

Write it as an article, not a transcript.

### Week 3: single-idea posts

Take three ideas from the talk and give each its own short post, spread across the week. Each post should make one point and stand on its own for someone who did not attend.

Good sources: a surprising data point, a mistake you described and what you learned, or an audience question and your answer.

If you have video, cut a short clip for one of them and add captions. Captions give people who cannot hear the audio access to the content [[9]](#ref-9) and help anyone watching with the sound off. YouTube can generate automatic captions, or set the timings for a transcript you type or upload [[10]](#ref-10). Check automatic captions first: W3C notes that one missed word, such as "not", can reverse the meaning [[9]](#ref-9). If you only have slides, use one clear slide as the image.

### Week 4: newsletter and profile update

Send a newsletter to your subscribers, or to your team or network if you do not have one. Summarise the talk in a few lines and link to the article and recording.

Then update your speaker record: add the talk with its title, date, organiser and links. This is what the next organiser will check. Our guide to [what organisers check in your speaker profile](/en/speaker-profile-what-organisers-check) explains what to include.

## A hypothetical example

For example, a product manager might give a 20-minute meetup talk on "Three pricing experiments that failed". Over the next month they could post a meetup photo with the lesson that surprised the room most, write up all three experiments with the audience's questions, give each experiment its own post with the key chart, and finish with a short newsletter and a profile update.

The recording then becomes the video sample in their next conference application [[2]](#ref-2).

## Common mistakes

- **Posting the slides without context.** On their own, slides rarely make sense.
- **Using material you do not have the right to share.** Check with the organiser about the recording, with the photographer about photos and with your employer about any internal data.
- **Selling in the follow-up.** If the organiser did not allow a sales pitch on stage, do not turn the follow-up content into one either.
- **Forgetting the next pitch.** Keep the recording, photos and abstract in one folder.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I repurpose a talk if it was not recorded?
Record your own audio on a phone if the organiser allows it, or write down your talk from your notes the same evening. The audience questions and your slides are often enough for an article and several posts.

### Can I post the organiser's recording of my talk?
Only if the organiser agrees. Ask when you confirm the slot, and ask whether you can share clips as well as the full video.

### Can I post photos where audience members are recognisable?
Be careful. Photos of identifiable people can be personal data, and IMY says posting them so that anyone can see them requires a legal basis [[5]](#ref-5) [[7]](#ref-7). The safest choice is a photo of you and your slides, or the organiser's official photos.

### Does repurposing help me get more speaking slots?
It can. Some organisers ask for a video of a past talk [[2]](#ref-2) or a photo, bio and abstract [[3]](#ref-3), and a captured talk gives you all of these.
