# How to get speaking opportunities: a practical guide for professionals

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

Most advice on getting speaking opportunities starts with conferences. That is the hardest door to open. Conference slots are few, calls for speakers are crowded, and many organisers want proof that you have spoken before.

There is a quieter route. Universities, business schools, student societies, alumni offices, professional associations and meetups all need outside speakers, and many of them say so publicly. This guide shows where those openings are, how to check that they are real, and a step-by-step process for getting your first and next invitations.

It is written for mid-level and senior professionals who want visibility, credibility and practice, not for people building a paid speaking business.

If you searched for how to get speaking gigs, the steps below are the ones you need. Just adjust your expectations: many of the venues in this guide pay no fee, so treat your first gigs as a way to earn visibility, references and practice.

## Key takeaways
- Start where demand is documented: university courses, alumni programmes, student societies, associations and meetups often publish an open invitation to speakers.
- Universities in Sweden are required by law to collaborate with the surrounding society, and many have alumni routes for guest lecturers.
- Students value guest speakers who work in the field, speak honestly and answer questions [[1]](#ref-1).
- Build a ladder: a small audience first, then a course, an association and finally a conference with a formal call for speakers.
- One clear topic, a short profile and a specific, polite pitch matter more than a long CV.

## Why the "hidden" venues are the best place to start

Conferences get the attention, but they are a small part of where talks happen. Universities are a good example. Swedish higher education institutions have a legal duty to "samverka med det omgivande samhället" (collaborate with the surrounding society) for mutual exchange [[2]](#ref-2). Guest lectures from practitioners are one everyday form of that.

Universities also say openly that they want practitioners. Stockholm University's alumni office writes that "guest lecturers that can share real-world experience are in high demand", both from students and academic advisors, and offers to match alumni with a suitable audience [[3]](#ref-3). KTH runs a register where alumni can sign up to give guest lectures [[4]](#ref-4). Business schools abroad do the same: Sheffield University Management School invites professionals to lead sessions that usually last 30 to 40 minutes plus 15 to 20 minutes of questions [[5]](#ref-5).

There is also evidence that students value these sessions. In a survey of 114 undergraduates published in the *Journal of the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning*, 86.1% of those who had taken a class with an industry guest speaker said they liked it. They valued speakers who currently worked in a related field, spoke with enthusiasm and honesty, and answered questions [[1]](#ref-1). The study is small and from one discipline (fashion), so treat it as an indication rather than a rule. Still, it describes exactly what most working professionals can offer.

### University courses and business schools
The strongest match is a specific course where your work is the subject. A head of payments fits a fintech course. A sustainability manager fits a course on corporate responsibility. Departments and alumni offices are the usual entry point. The University of Edinburgh Business School, for example, lists "on-course speaking" and alumni panels as options and asks interested alumni to email the alumni team with their area of expertise [[6]](#ref-6). Cambridge Judge Business School runs practitioner speaker series for its EMBA and Master of Finance students [[7]](#ref-7).

### Student societies
Student-run societies book speakers for their own events. At the Stockholm School of Economics, the student Entrepreneurship Society runs Initiate, "a series of inspirational lectures by entrepreneurs", and invites people to ask about speaking [[8]](#ref-8). Societies change boards often, so check the current contacts on their own site before you write.

### Alumni programmes
If you studied at a university, start there. Stockholm University, KTH and Uppsala University all list guest lecturing as a way for alumni to get involved [[3]](#ref-3) [[4]](#ref-4) [[9]](#ref-9). Your shared history gives you a natural reason to write.

### Associations and chambers of commerce
Associations run seminars, breakfasts and panels all year. AmCham Sweden lists fireside chats among its events [[10]](#ref-10). These audiences are peers and decision-makers, which makes them valuable once you have a few talks behind you.

### Meetups
Many meetups openly ask for speakers. AWS User Group Stockholm says "Speakers are always welcome!", accepts talks at any experience level, and takes proposals through a call-for-proposals form [[11]](#ref-11). Meetups are the lowest-risk place to test a talk.

### Conferences
Conferences use formal calls with deadlines and selection criteria. DevSum in Stockholm, for example, closes its call for speakers on 8 December 2026 for an event on 27–28 May 2027, and covers travel and accommodation for speakers [[12]](#ref-12). Expect competition, and expect organisers to ask what you have spoken at before.

## A step-by-step process

### Step 1: Pick one topic you can defend
Choose a topic where you have first-hand experience, not just opinions. Write it as one sentence: who it is for and what they will understand afterwards. For example (hypothetical), "How a mid-sized bank decides which fintech partners to work with, for final-year finance students."

### Step 2: Prepare a short speaker profile
Organisers check who you are before they reply. You need a two- to four-line bio, a current photo, one or two talk titles, and a link where they can verify your role. Our guide to [what organisers check in a speaker profile](/en/speaker-profile-what-organisers-check) has a full checklist.

### Step 3: Make a target list of 15–25 venues
Mix the six venue types above. For universities, look for named courses where your topic fits. For societies, associations and meetups, look for evidence that they host outside speakers: a past events list, a speaker series or an explicit invitation. If you cannot find such evidence, move on.

### Step 4: Find the right person or route
The right contact is rarely a general inbox. For a course it is usually the course coordinator. For alumni programmes it is the alumni office, which often publishes a functional address. For meetups and conferences, use the official form. Use the route the organisation itself publishes.

### Step 5: Write a short, specific pitch
Say who you are, which course or event you mean, what you would cover, and what the audience gets. Offer a format and a time window. Keep it under 150 words. Write in the organiser's language. See [how to email a course coordinator](/en/guest-lecture-email-templates) for templates.

### Step 6: Follow up once, then let it go
Send one polite follow-up after a week or two. If there is still no reply, do not write to other people in the same organisation to get around the person you contacted. It damages trust and rarely works.

### Step 7: Deliver, then ask for the next step
After the talk, ask the organiser for brief feedback and whether they know another course, society or event that could use the same talk. One good session often leads to the next.

## How to move up the ladder

Think of your first year as a sequence, not a jackpot. A meetup or society talk gives you practice and a first reference. A course lecture gives you a credible line in your bio. An association seminar puts you in front of peers. After that, a conference application has something to stand on. Our [12-month speaking plan](/en/first-talk-to-keynote-12-month-plan) breaks this into months.

## Common mistakes

- **Pitching your company instead of a topic.** brightonSEO, for example, tells applicants to avoid self-promotion such as pitching their own tool or client results [[13]](#ref-13).
- **Writing to a generic address when a named route exists.** Use the route the organisation publishes.
- **Offering a talk with no audience in mind.** "I can talk about leadership" is not a pitch. "Ninety minutes on leading through a reorganisation, for your organisational behaviour course" is.
- **Ignoring timing.** University teaching is planned per term. Pitch before a course is scheduled, not the week it runs.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need speaking experience to get my first opportunity?
No. Meetups, student societies and alumni programmes are open to first-time speakers, and some say so explicitly. AWS User Group Stockholm accepts talks at any experience level [[11]](#ref-11). Use those first talks to build references.

### Will I get paid for guest lectures?
Do not expect it. Most of the university and alumni pages we checked do not mention payment at all. Treat guest lectures as a way to gain visibility, credibility and practice, and ask about expenses before you accept.

### Who should I contact at a university?
For a specific course, the course coordinator is usually the right person. If you are an alumnus or alumna, the alumni office is a good first route, because several universities publish an address for exactly this purpose [[3]](#ref-3) [[9]](#ref-9).

### How many pitches should I send?
Quality beats volume. A list of 15 to 25 well-matched venues, each with a tailored pitch and one follow-up, is a realistic start. Avoid sending several pitches to the same organiser in a short period.
