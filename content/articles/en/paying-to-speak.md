# Paying to speak: when it is worth it and how to spot a bad deal

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

Some speaking slots cost money. Sometimes that is open and fair: a sponsor buys visibility, or an academic conference charges every presenter a registration fee. Sometimes it is a poor deal dressed up as an honour, or an outright scam.

This guide explains the main ways speakers end up paying, when paying can make sense, and a checklist of red flags. We include real, published examples, with the date we checked them. Spoke At shows the cost of an opportunity only when it is published by the organiser, so you can decide for yourself.

## Key takeaways

- "Paying to speak" covers several different things: sponsor packages with a speaking slot, fees charged to selected speakers, academic registration fees and predatory events.
- Many good events charge speakers nothing, and some cover travel and hotels.
- Paying can make sense when it is a marketing decision with a clear audience, or a normal academic registration for a conference you would attend anyway.
- For an individual building credibility, a bought slot is usually a weaker signal than one won on merit.
- Walk away if the fee appears after you say yes, the selection process is unclear, or the invitation is flattering but vague.

## The forms paying to speak takes

| Model | What you pay | Published example |
|---|---|---|
| Free, costs covered | Nothing; travel and hotel paid | DevSum 2027, Stockholm: free for speakers, travel and accommodation expenses covered [[1]](#ref-1) |
| Free, own costs | Nothing to the organiser; you pay travel | Accelerate Tomorrow Summit 2027: free, selected on merit, two-day pass, no fee, travel or hotel [[2]](#ref-2) |
| Selected, then pay to attend | A discounted attendance fee | Money20/20 Europe 2027: selected applicants are offered attendance at €2,495 + VAT, and the suggesting company covers its speakers' costs [[3]](#ref-3) |
| Academic registration | The conference fee, like every participant | PROFES 2026 at Blekinge Institute of Technology: authors of accepted papers must register; full conference SEK 6,700 early bird, SEK 7,800 after [[4]](#ref-4) |
| Sponsorship package with a speaking role | A sponsorship fee | DIGITALEUROPE's 2026–2027 events brochure lists a €35,000 Gold package with a "speaking opportunity for a C-level executive", and €50,000 award sponsorships that include a "speaking role" [[5]](#ref-5) |
| Predatory event | A fee in exchange for "acceptance" regardless of merit | See the section below [[6]](#ref-6) |

These examples are not ranked or endorsed. They show the range. Prices were checked on 16 November 2026 and can change.

### Universities and academic conferences

Paying to present is standard at many academic conferences, including those hosted by universities. At PROFES 2026, hosted in Karlskrona at Blekinge Institute of Technology, each accepted paper must have a unique registered presenter [[4]](#ref-4). At NORKOM 2025, listed under the University of Gothenburg's School of Public Administration, the participation fee was SEK 2,250, and the page says all participants must register, even co-authors of a paper [[7]](#ref-7).

This is not a scam. It is how academic conferences fund themselves, and every participant pays. But it matters if you are a practitioner who expects that "speaking at a university conference" is free. A guest lecture in a course is a different thing: you are invited into teaching, and you should not be asked to pay for that.

Whether a guest lecture comes with a fee or travel expenses is a separate question, covered in [do guest lecturers get paid?](/en/do-guest-lecturers-get-paid)

## When paying can be worth it

**When it is a marketing decision.** If your company sells to exactly the people in the room, a sponsored slot is advertising. Judge it like any other marketing spend: cost per relevant attendee, lead quality, and whether the slot will be labelled as sponsored. Some events keep sponsors and speakers apart. The Accelerate Tomorrow Summit, for example, says its sessions focus on "experience and insight, not sales pitches" and sends companies that want to showcase a product to its partnership route [[2]](#ref-2).

**When it is a normal academic registration.** If you have a paper accepted at a peer-reviewed conference in your field, and you would attend anyway, the registration fee is the price of taking part.

**When you are selected first and then pay to attend.** At events like Money20/20 Europe, you pitch, a team selects, and selected speakers are offered a reduced attendance rate [[3]](#ref-3). Compare that with the ticket price you would pay to attend anyway, and with the value of the audience to your business.

## When it is a bad deal

**When you want credibility as an individual.** The point of a talk for credibility is that someone independent chose you. A bought slot removes that signal. If the programme labels the session as sponsored, it will be read as a sponsor message.

**When the fee is the selection process.** If anyone who pays gets on stage, the stage tells your audience nothing about you.

**When the audience is unclear.** If the organiser cannot say who attends, how many and in which roles, you cannot judge value.

**When there are good free alternatives.** Many events charge speakers nothing. Nordic Tech Days 2027 covers travel up to €350 (or €500 transatlantic) plus two hotel nights for speakers [[8]](#ref-8). Guest lectures, student society talks and meetups are other routes worth trying before you pay. Our guide on [how to get speaking opportunities](/en/how-to-get-speaking-opportunities) covers those routes.

## Predatory conferences: the extreme case

Predatory conferences are events that imitate legitimate meetings but accept submissions largely for the fee. A 2022 report by the InterAcademy Partnership, a global network of science academies, describes predatory outlets as "motivated by profit rather than scholarship" [[9]](#ref-9). In its global survey of more than 1,800 researchers, over 80% said predatory journals and conferences were already a problem or on the rise in their country, and the study estimates that over a million researchers have likely used predatory outlets, mostly without knowing [[9]](#ref-9).

An opinion piece in the science news outlet Eos summarises the same survey: 4% of respondents had attended a predatory conference, 1% knowingly and 3% unaware [[6]](#ref-6). It also notes that the U.S. Federal Trade Commission took action against OMICS Group, and that a federal court ruled in 2019 that its peer-review claims were false and ordered more than $50 million in payments [[6]](#ref-6). The author works for a company that sells conference software, which the article discloses.

Most of this research is about academic conferences. We have not found comparable data for business events, but the same checks apply to any speaking invitation that comes with a fee.

## Red flag checklist

The University of Borås library advises checking the invitation, the organiser's reputation, the subject area, the review process and the website before you accept [[10]](#ref-10). It describes predatory actors as using false or misleading information and aggressive, indiscriminate solicitation [[10]](#ref-10). Combined with the warning signs in the Eos article [[6]](#ref-6), that gives a practical list:

| Red flag | What to do |
|---|---|
| Unsolicited, flattering invitation to an event outside your field | Ask how they found you and why your topic fits |
| The fee appears only after you say yes | Ask for all costs in writing before you commit |
| No visible selection process or programme committee | Ask who reviews talks and how many submissions they accept |
| Vague venue, agenda or audience | Ask for last year's programme and attendee profile |
| The organiser does not answer questions, or answers evasively | Treat silence as a no |
| Sponsors or partners unrelated to the topic | Check the sponsors' own sites |
| A website address not linked to a known organisation | Check who owns the event and whether past editions happened |
| Pressure to decide fast ("last keynote slot") | Take a week anyway |
| "Keynote" or "award" that comes with a package price | Treat it as advertising, and price it as advertising |
| Rights to your recording or slides hidden in the terms | Read what will be published and where; good calls state this openly |

## Questions to ask before you pay

1. Exactly what do I pay, and what is included (ticket, travel, hotel, recording)?
2. Will the session be labelled as sponsored in the programme?
3. How are speakers selected, and by whom?
4. Who attended last time, by role and number?
5. Can I speak to a past speaker?
6. What is the cancellation and refund policy?
7. What happens to my slides and the recording?

If the answers are clear and the numbers work, paying can be a sound business decision. If they are vague, walk away. Before you spend money, also read our [guide to finding open calls for speakers](/en/call-for-speakers-guide), where many calls are free.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it normal to pay to speak at a conference?
It depends on the type of event. Many industry and tech conferences charge speakers nothing, and some cover travel. Many academic conferences require presenters to register and pay the conference fee. Sponsored speaking slots are sold as advertising.

### Do university conferences charge speakers?
Many academic conferences hosted by universities require presenting authors to register and pay, like any participant. PROFES 2026 at Blekinge Institute of Technology, for example, requires each accepted paper to have a registered presenter. A guest lecture in a university course is different and should not cost you anything.

### Is a sponsored keynote worth the money?
Only if you judge it as marketing. Work out the cost per relevant attendee and check whether the slot will be labelled as sponsored. If you want credibility rather than leads, a talk selected on merit is a stronger signal.

### How can I tell if a conference is predatory?
Warning signs include unsolicited invitations outside your field, fees that appear after acceptance, no clear review process, vague venues or agendas, and organisers who do not answer questions. Check the organiser's history and whether past editions actually took place.
