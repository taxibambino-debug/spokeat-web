# Your speaker profile: what organisers check before they say yes

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

When you pitch a talk, the organiser does not just read your email. They look you up. A course coordinator wants to know if you can teach their students something real. A student society wants a name that will fill a room. A conference programme team wants proof that you have something new to say and will not sell from the stage.

Your speaker profile is what they find. It is usually a short bio, a photo, a few talk titles and a link or two. This article turns what organisers actually publish about their selection into a checklist you can work through in an evening.

## Key takeaways
- Organisers check three things: are you credible on this topic, do you fit this audience, and will you deliver without selling.
- Students rate guest speakers higher when they work in a related field, speak honestly and answer questions [[1]](#ref-1). Your profile should show the first and promise the rest.
- Tailor the bio for each event. Sessionize, a common call-for-speakers platform, advises explaining why your experience matches that particular event [[2]](#ref-2).
- Speaker agencies ask for video and a list of past talks [[3]](#ref-3). None of the university speaker pages we checked asked for either.
- Keep one master profile and cut it down per pitch.

## What different organisers look for

Organisers rarely publish a full checklist. But many say what they want in their invitations and calls for speakers. Here is what the pages we checked ask for.

| Organiser type | What they say they want | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Business school (guest lecture) | Insight into your current role, your organisation and your career journey | Sheffield University Management School [[4]](#ref-4) |
| Business school alumni team | Your area of expertise, sent by email | University of Edinburgh Business School [[5]](#ref-5) |
| Executive education speaker series | Practitioners who can talk about the challenges of running an organisation, or about topical deals and business problems they have faced | Cambridge Judge Business School [[6]](#ref-6) |
| Practitioner conference | A specific title, a description, the ideal audience and what attendees will take away; no self-promotion | brightonSEO [[7]](#ref-7) |
| Large industry conference | First-hand experience, a clear point of view, original research or case studies; no sales pitches | Money20/20 Europe [[8]](#ref-8) |
| Speaker agency | Biography, topics with descriptions, recent video, fee, list of past engagements | Leading Authorities [[3]](#ref-3) |

The pattern is clear. The further you move from a classroom towards a paid stage, the more proof organisers want. For a first guest lecture, a credible role and a clear topic usually carry the pitch. For a large conference, you need evidence that you have done this before.

## The checklist

### 1. A one-line positioning statement
One sentence that says what you do and what you can speak about. For example (hypothetical): "Head of risk at a Nordic payments company, speaking on how fraud teams use transaction data." This is what a busy coordinator will remember.

### 2. A short bio, in two lengths
Write a 50-word version for emails and a 120-word version for event pages. Lead with your current role, then the experience that makes you credible on this topic, then one human detail if it helps. Skip long lists of past titles.

Sessionize's own guidance for speakers is to point out "why your knowledge and experience make you a perfect match for the event" [[2]](#ref-2). A generic bio pasted into every form does not do that.

### 3. Proof that you work in the field now
Students in the guest-speaker study valued speakers who currently worked in a field related to the course and who shared insider information about their day-to-day work [[1]](#ref-1). Make your current role easy to verify. A professional profile page or a company page that names you is enough.

### 4. Two or three talk titles with one-line descriptions
Organisers think in sessions, not in people. Give them sessions. Each title should name a specific problem. brightonSEO advises against broad topics such as "social media marketing" and asks for narrow, practical talks [[7]](#ref-7). For each title, add one line on who it is for and what they will take away.

### 5. Audience fit, stated plainly
Say which audiences you have in mind: first-year undergraduates, master's students, a society's members, peers in an industry association. A course coordinator will relax if you show you understand the level of their students. For associations, see [how to speak at industry associations and chambers](/en/speak-at-industry-associations-and-chambers).

### 6. A current, professional photo
Sessionize stores a photo with your speaker profile and reuses it for new submissions [[2]](#ref-2), so the same picture will follow you from event to event. Use a recent, well-lit head-and-shoulders picture. It does not need to be a studio shot.

### 7. Speaking history, even if short
List what you have done, newest first. Internal training sessions, team workshops, a meetup or a panel all count. If you have not spoken yet, leave this out rather than inflate it. Agencies ask for a list of engagements in the past year [[3]](#ref-3); most course coordinators will not, but a short list still helps.

### 8. Evidence you will not sell from the stage
This is a top concern for practitioner conferences. Money20/20 Europe says it does not want "sales pitches or recycled talking points" [[8]](#ref-8), and brightonSEO asks speakers to avoid pitching their own tool or client results [[7]](#ref-7). In your profile, describe topics, not products. If you work for a vendor, say so, and show that the talk is about the problem, not the product.

### 9. Video or written work, if you have it
A short clip from any talk, or a few articles you have written, lets organisers judge how you explain things. Speaker agencies expect recent video [[3]](#ref-3). For a guest lecture it is a bonus, not a requirement. Writing also builds the kind of reputation we discuss in [how speaking builds industry authority](/en/speaking-builds-industry-authority).

### 10. A contact route that works
Use an email address you check. Sessionize notes that the email field is where the organiser will contact you about your submission [[2]](#ref-2). Missed replies are a common and avoidable loss.

## Matching your profile to the opportunity

The same profile does not fit every venue. Before each pitch, ask three questions.

- **Am I eligible?** Some programmes are only for alumni. KTH's guest lecture register, for example, is aimed at alumni [[9]](#ref-9). Some conferences ask regular speakers to make room for new voices [[8]](#ref-8). Check before you write.
- **Does my topic fit this audience?** A course has learning outcomes. A society has members with specific interests. An association has a theme for its season.
- **Can I show it in two lines?** If the link between your experience and their audience takes a paragraph to explain, pick a better-fitting venue.

If you are not sure where you fit, start with the [practical guide to getting speaking opportunities](/en/how-to-get-speaking-opportunities), which maps the main venue types.

## A simple speaker page

Organisers look you up, so give them one page to land on. It can be a page on your own site or a well-kept LinkedIn profile. It does not need a designer. It needs to answer the organiser's questions in under a minute.

What to put on it:

- **Name, current role and a photo.** The same as in your pitch emails.
- **Two or three talk titles**, each with one line on who it is for and what they will take away.
- **Your bio in two lengths**, ready to copy into a programme.
- **Past talks**, with the venue, the date and a link if the organiser published the session.
- **Short quotes from organisers**, if you have permission (see the next section).
- **A contact route** you check.

If you submit to conferences, a platform profile can do part of this job. Sessionize, for example, lets speakers keep a public profile on the platform [[10]](#ref-10). Keep it consistent with your own page, so an organiser who finds both sees the same person. Our guide on [building your personal brand through speaking](/en/personal-brand-through-speaking) covers how the page fits with your LinkedIn profile and talks over time.

## References and testimonials

A line from an organiser carries more weight than anything you write about yourself. The simplest way to get one is to ask soon after the talk, while the session is fresh.

1. **Thank the organiser first.** Keep the thank-you and the request in the same short email, or send the request a few days later.
2. **Ask for one or two sentences.** For example: "Would you be willing to write a sentence or two about the session that I could quote on my speaker page?"
3. **Ask for permission explicitly.** Confirm how they want to be named: full name and role, role only, or the organisation only.
4. **Do not write the quote for them, and do not change its meaning.** You can fix a typo with their agreement, nothing more.
5. **Ask if you may name them as a reference.** Some organisers are happy to take a short call from the next organiser. Others are not, and that is fine.

Keep the quotes, with the date and the permission, in one place so you can use them later. Our checklist for [what to do after your talk](/en/after-your-talk) covers the thank-you note, feedback and asking for the next invitation.

## Common profile mistakes

- **A CV instead of a bio.** Organisers want relevance, not every job you have had.
- **Vague titles.** "Leadership insights" tells a coordinator nothing.
- **Outdated role.** If your profile says one job and your email signature another, it raises doubts.
- **Overclaiming.** Calling yourself a "keynote speaker" after one panel is easy to check and easy to see through.
- **Company slides as a portfolio.** Sales decks signal exactly what conference teams say they reject.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a speaker bio be?
Keep two versions: about 50 words for pitch emails and about 120 words for event pages. Lead with your current role and the experience that makes you credible on the topic. Cut anything that does not help the organiser decide.

### Do I need a showreel to get booked?
Not for most first opportunities. None of the university or business school speaker pages we checked asked for video. Speaker agencies do, so record your talks when you can and build a short clip over time [[3]](#ref-3).

### Should I mention my company in my speaker profile?
Yes, your role and employer are part of your credibility. But describe your talks by the problem they address, not by your product. Several conferences say they reject sales pitches [[7]](#ref-7) [[8]](#ref-8).

### What if I have never spoken before?
Leave the speaking history out and lead with your work. A clear topic and a credible current role are enough for many guest lectures and meetup talks. Add each talk to your profile as you go.
