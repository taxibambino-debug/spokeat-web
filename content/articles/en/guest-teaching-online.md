# Guest teaching online: speaking in distance courses and online programmes

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

An online guest lecture removes the biggest barrier to teaching: travel. You can speak to students on another continent in the morning and be back in a meeting by lunch. For a busy professional, that makes guest teaching realistic several times a year.

But online teaching is not one thing. Some distance courses have live seminars every week. Others are entirely self-paced, with no scheduled sessions at all. If you pitch the second kind, there is simply no slot for you.

This guide explains which online formats invite guests and which do not, how to read a course page to tell the difference, how to deal with time zones, and how to run a session that works on screen.

## Key takeaways
- Online guest lectures save travel and open up universities worldwide, but they only work in courses with live or scheduled teaching.
- Self-paced distance courses based on individual work usually have no slot for a guest [[1]](#ref-1).
- Some universities run online guest lecture schemes. RMIT Europe offers a 15-minute Q&A or a 30-minute talk, live or pre-recorded [[2]](#ref-2).
- Time zones are the main practical problem. A pre-recorded talk plus a short live Q&A can solve it.
- Ask before the session whether it will be recorded and where the recording will be used.

## What changes when the lecture is online

Three things are different from a campus lecture.

**No travel.** You can say yes to a course in another city or country without taking a day off. That makes it easier to accept one-off invitations and to build relationships with several universities.

**A global audience.** Online teaching can put you in front of students far away. RMIT Europe, for example, connects European professionals with RMIT students in Australia and Vietnam [[2]](#ref-2).

**Less room for improvisation.** On screen you cannot read the room as easily. Students may have cameras off. Questions come in the chat. You need more structure, shorter segments and someone to manage the questions.

Students' expectations are similar either way. In a survey of 114 undergraduates about industry guest speakers, students valued guests who worked in a related field, spoke with enthusiasm and candour, answered questions, and talked about their daily work and career advice [[3]](#ref-3). The study looked at classroom speakers, so treat it as a guide rather than evidence about online sessions specifically. But the same qualities are easy to deliver through a camera.

## Which online formats invite guests, and which do not

| Format | Live sessions? | Room for a guest? | How to approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distance course with scheduled online seminars | Yes | Good | Contact the course coordinator |
| Hybrid course with online participation | Yes | Good | Contact the course coordinator |
| Online guest lecture scheme at a university | Yes, or recorded | Good | Use the scheme's contact |
| Alumni programme with online options | Varies | Good for alumni | Contact the alumni office |
| Online webinar series run by a company or community | Yes | Good, but not a course | Apply through their form |
| Self-paced distance course based on individual work | No | Little or none | Usually not worth a pitch |

### Formats that invite guests

**Distance courses with live teaching.** Some distance courses have scheduled online sessions, and the course page often tells you so. Uppsala University's course "3R for Sustainability Managers", for example, is fully web-based and requires students to have a computer with speakers, a microphone and a camera [[4]](#ref-4). A requirement like that suggests live interaction, which is where a guest can fit.

**Courses taught in the evening.** Some distance courses are designed for people who work. Uppsala University lists its course "A Changing Baltic Sea: Environment, Society and Sustainability" as distance learning with evening instructional time [[5]](#ref-5). An evening slot is often easier for a working guest speaker too. Course pages are not always complete, so confirm how the sessions run with the course coordinator.

**University schemes for online guest lectures.** Some universities actively recruit online guests. RMIT Europe invites industry professionals to give online guest lectures to RMIT students. First, RMIT connects you with a course coordinator in your field to discuss the course. Then you agree the format, timing and date. The options are a 15-minute Q&A with students, facilitated by the course coordinator, or a 30-minute presentation, pre-recorded or live [[2]](#ref-2).

**Alumni programmes with online options.** The University of Edinburgh Business School says its alumni speaker opportunities run both in person and online [[6]](#ref-6). If you studied abroad, your old university's alumni office may be the easiest online route.

**Webinar series.** These are not courses, but they are a useful adjacent format. Data Science Dojo, for example, runs one-hour Zoom webinars with a 30 to 45-minute presentation and up to 15 minutes of questions. Sessions are live-streamed and the recordings are published afterwards [[7]](#ref-7).

### Formats that rarely invite guests

**Self-paced courses based on individual work.** Some distance courses have no scheduled meetings at all. Uppsala University's distance course "Academic Spanish: Theory and Practice" says students work individually and complete assignments via the internet [[1]](#ref-1). There is no session where a guest could appear. You could offer a recorded talk, but the teacher would have to want to add one to the course material.

**Courses with no teaching slot this term.** Even a course with live seminars may have all sessions planned already. Read our guide to the [academic calendar for guest lecturers](/en/academic-calendar-guest-lectures) to time your pitch.

## How to read a course page

Course pages often tell you more than you expect. Look for these fields:

- **Mode of study.** "Distance", "online" or "hybrid". Uppsala's course search, for example, lets you filter on campus or distance [[8]](#ref-8).
- **Instructional time.** Day, evening or mixed.
- **Mandatory meetings.** Zero on-campus meetings does not mean zero online sessions. Read on.
- **Technical requirements.** A requirement for a camera and microphone suggests live sessions [[4]](#ref-4).
- **Teaching description.** Words like "seminars", "web lectures" or "online discussions" are good signs. "Individual work" and "self-study" are not [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Language of instruction.** Make sure you can teach in it.

If the page is unclear, ask the course coordinator one simple question: "Does the course have live online sessions where a guest could join?"

## Time zones: the practical problem

The more global the programme, the harder it is to find a time that suits everyone. When it is morning in one part of the world, it can be night in another.

Ways to handle it:

- **State times in both zones.** Write "14:00 Stockholm time (CET)" rather than just "2 pm".
- **Offer several windows.** Two or three possible times give the coordinator room to plan.
- **Combine recorded and live.** Record a short talk in advance and join only for a live Q&A. RMIT Europe offers exactly this kind of split, with a pre-recorded or live presentation and a separate Q&A option [[2]](#ref-2).
- **Expect organisers to have a default slot.** Data Science Dojo usually holds its webinars on Wednesdays at noon Pacific time but says it can adjust to the presenter's schedule. It also sets dates 4 to 6 weeks ahead for marketing [[7]](#ref-7).

## Running the session on screen

- **Test the platform.** Ask which tool the course uses and test your sound and camera in advance.
- **Plan short segments.** Break the talk into parts of a few minutes each, with a question or example between them.
- **Ask for a moderator.** Let the teacher manage the chat and read out questions. At RMIT, the Q&A option is facilitated by the course coordinator [[2]](#ref-2).
- **Ask about recording.** Find out if the session will be recorded, who will see it and for how long. Some organisers publish recordings openly [[7]](#ref-7).
- **Share materials after.** Offer slides or links for the course platform.

## Presenting on camera in Teams or Zoom

The course decides the tool. Teams and Zoom are common, but the basics of looking and sounding clear are the same in any of them. University teaching-support pages agree on most of it.

**Camera at eye level.** Raise the camera to the height of your eyes. Stockholm University's teaching centre puts it bluntly: otherwise participants spend the session looking up your nostrils [[10]](#ref-10). A pile of books under a laptop works, as Louisiana State University's guide suggests [[9]](#ref-9).

**Sound first.** Stockholm University's guidance calls sound the single most important factor in hybrid teaching [[10]](#ref-10). Close the door, use a headset or earbuds with a built-in microphone, and speak at a conversational volume and pace [[9]](#ref-9). Do a short sound check with the teacher before students join.

**Light on your face, not behind you.** Sit facing a window or another soft light source, and avoid having a bright window behind you [[9]](#ref-9) [[10]](#ref-10). Keep the background simple enough that students look at you rather than your bookshelf [[9]](#ref-9).

**Build in interaction.** Students on screen are easy to forget. Plan for it:

- **Chat.** Ask a question and let students answer in the chat. Uppsala University's Zoom guidance notes that students can ask questions by microphone or chat [[11]](#ref-11). Ask the teacher, or a student, to watch the chat and raised hands for you, as Stockholm University suggests [[10]](#ref-10).
- **Polls.** A quick poll at the start tells you what the group already knows. Zoom has a poll function, and Uppsala and Stockholm University both point teachers to it [[11]](#ref-11) [[10]](#ref-10). Agree with the teacher in advance who sets it up.
- **Fixed question points.** Stop every few minutes for questions, rather than only at the end.

Do a full test run in the same tool, from the same room, a day or two before. Camera, sound and light problems are much easier to fix when nobody is waiting.

## How to pitch an online guest lecture

Pitch it like a campus lecture, with three additions:

1. Say that you can teach online and name the time zone you are in.
2. Mention whether you could pre-record part of the session.
3. Link your topic to a specific week or module in the course.

For example, a hypothetical sustainability reporting manager in Stockholm might write to the coordinator of a web-based course on corporate sustainability reporting. She would offer a 30-minute live session on how her company prepares its reports, tied to the module on regulations, with a recorded version as a fallback.

For the basics of finding the right course and contact, read [how to become a guest lecturer at a university](/en/become-a-guest-lecturer-at-a-university).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do universities accept guest lecturers online?
Some do, and a few run dedicated schemes. RMIT Europe recruits industry professionals for online guest lectures [[2]](#ref-2), and the University of Edinburgh Business School offers online alumni speaker opportunities [[6]](#ref-6). For other courses, ask the course coordinator.

### Is a recorded guest lecture as good as a live one?
A recording is easier to schedule but loses the live questions, which students value [[3]](#ref-3). A short recording plus a live Q&A gives you both.

### How long is an online guest lecture?
It varies. RMIT Europe offers a 15-minute Q&A or a 30-minute presentation [[2]](#ref-2). Webinar formats such as Data Science Dojo's run for one hour including questions [[7]](#ref-7).

### Are online guest lectures paid?
Usually not. Data Science Dojo states that it does not pay speakers [[7]](#ref-7), and most university pages do not mention payment. Treat it as a way to gain visibility and practice.
