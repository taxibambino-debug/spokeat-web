# When fear of speaking needs professional help (glossophobia)

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)


> **If you are in crisis:** if you or someone you know is in immediate danger or thinking about suicide, call your local emergency number now. In Sweden, call 112. In the US, you can call or text 988.

Most people feel nervous before speaking in front of others. For some, the fear is much stronger. It shapes career choices, makes meetings an ordeal, or leads to weeks of dread before a single presentation.

This page explains how to tell ordinary nerves from fear that may need professional support, what clinical guidelines describe, and where to get help. It is general information. It cannot diagnose you, and it does not replace an assessment by a doctor or psychologist.

## Key takeaways
- "Glossophobia" is an informal name for the fear of public speaking. Health services such as the NHS, NIMH and Sweden's 1177 describe this kind of fear under social anxiety (social phobia).
- Feeling nervous is normal. It becomes a reason to seek help when the fear is strong, persistent and stops you doing things you want or need to do.
- The UK's NICE guideline recommends a specific form of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) as the first treatment for adults with social anxiety disorder.
- Research shows psychological treatments reduce the fear of public speaking, and online formats appear to work about as well as face-to-face for many people.
- Your first contact is usually a GP or primary care provider. In Sweden, that is a vårdcentral or 1177.

## Glossophobia, nerves and social anxiety: what is the difference?

"Glossophobia" is a word you will see online, but it is not the term most health services use. The NHS describes social anxiety disorder, also called social phobia, as "a long-term and overwhelming fear of social situations" [[1]](#ref-1). The US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) notes that for some people, the anxiety "may arise only during performance situations such as giving a speech" [[2]](#ref-2). Sweden's public health portal 1177 likewise says that for some people the discomfort is tied to one situation, "for example speaking in front of a group" (our translation from Swedish) [[3]](#ref-3).

In short, three things are often mixed up:

| | Ordinary nerves | Strong fear of speaking | Social anxiety disorder |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it feels like | Butterflies, faster heartbeat, some worry | Intense fear, often long before the talk | Intense, persistent fear of being judged |
| Effect on life | Uncomfortable, but you do it anyway | May lead you to avoid some opportunities | Gets in the way of work, studies or relationships |
| Who decides | You | You, possibly with professional input | Only a qualified professional can assess this |

The middle column is not a diagnosis. It is a reminder that fear exists on a scale. 1177 puts it this way: everyone can feel shy or nervous sometimes, but in social anxiety the nervousness is "so strong and recurring that it holds you back in everyday life" (our translation) [[3]](#ref-3).

### How common is it?

A large US survey estimated that 12.1% of people meet the criteria for social phobia at some point in their life [[4]](#ref-4). In a Swedish population survey, public speaking was the most common social fear [[5]](#ref-5). These figures come from different methods and countries, so treat them as indications, not exact numbers.

## Signs it may be time to seek help

Consider talking to a healthcare professional if one or more of these apply to you:

- The fear is **persistent**: it has lasted a long time and does not ease with experience.
- You **avoid** opportunities: you turn down presentations, promotions, courses or social events because of it.
- You have **strong physical reactions**, such as panic attacks. The NHS lists physical symptoms such as sweating, a racing heartbeat and panic attacks among the signs of social anxiety [[1]](#ref-1).
- You **worry for days or weeks** before speaking, or go over it for a long time afterwards.
- You use **alcohol or drugs** to cope. 1177 specifically says this is a reason to seek help [[3]](#ref-3).
- You feel **low or hopeless**. The NHS notes that social anxiety often occurs alongside depression or other anxiety problems [[1]](#ref-1).

You do not need to be sure you "have" anything before you ask for help. Describing what you experience is enough.

## What happens when you seek help

NIMH explains that a health care provider may ask about your symptoms and may do a physical examination to rule out other causes [[2]](#ref-2). From there, they may offer treatment themselves or refer you on, for example to a psychologist.

Bring concrete examples. Which situations trigger the fear? What do you avoid? How long has it been going on? What have you already tried?

## What guidelines and research describe

This section summarises what official guidance and research say. It is not a treatment recommendation for you. Your options depend on an assessment by a professional.

**Clinical guidance.** The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) says the first treatment adults with social anxiety disorder should be offered is "a psychological therapy called cognitive behavioural therapy", usually individual and specifically developed for social anxiety [[6]](#ref-6). If someone does not want that, NICE describes supported self-help based on CBT as an alternative. It notes that antidepressants "are usually not as helpful as CBT for social anxiety disorder" and are not the first option [[6]](#ref-6). The NHS also describes CBT as "generally considered the best treatment" [[1]](#ref-1).

**Research on fear of public speaking.** A 2019 meta-analysis of 30 randomised controlled trials with 1,355 participants found that psychological interventions reduced the fear of public speaking, and that technology-assisted formats (such as online or virtual-reality programmes) appeared about as effective as face-to-face treatment [[7]](#ref-7). The authors also reported some indications of publication bias, which means the true effect may be smaller [[7]](#ref-7).

**Barriers to getting help.** Reporting on that research, ScienceNorway noted that nearly half of people with social anxiety disorder do not contact a psychologist, in part because the therapy setting itself causes them great anxiety [[8]](#ref-8). If that sounds like you, it may help to know that online and guided self-help formats exist. 1177 notes that CBT is also available online in Sweden [[3]](#ref-3).

Another barrier is mistaking the problem for ordinary shyness. In a review in the Swedish medical journal Läkartidningen, the authors write that social phobia is often seen as shyness that will pass, which is one reason few people seek help. They add that prospective studies suggest the opposite: few improve on their own [[9]](#ref-9).

## Where to get help

### In the UK
- **Your GP.** The NHS says it is a good idea to see a GP if you think you have social anxiety [[1]](#ref-1).
- **NHS talking therapies.** You can refer yourself directly without a GP referral [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Emergencies.** If your life is at risk, call your local emergency number.

### In the US
- **A health care provider**, such as a primary care doctor, psychiatrist, psychologist or clinical social worker [[2]](#ref-2).
- **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.** Call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org. In life-threatening situations, call 911 [[2]](#ref-2).

### In Sweden
- **Vårdcentral (health centre)** or psychiatric outpatient clinic. 1177 notes that all health centres have access to a counsellor or psychologist [[3]](#ref-3).
- **1177** by phone for healthcare advice, or 1177.se for information [[3]](#ref-3).
- **Psychiatric emergency department** if you have thoughts of taking your own life. If there is immediate danger to life, call **112** [[3]](#ref-3).

### Elsewhere
Contact your GP or primary care provider, or your local emergency number if you are in danger.

## If you are supporting someone else

1177 suggests listening without judging, encouraging the person to seek help, cheering them on in small steps, and respecting it when they say no. It also reminds family and friends to look after their own health [[3]](#ref-3).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is glossophobia?
Glossophobia is an informal name for the fear of public speaking. Health services usually describe it under social anxiety (social phobia), which can be limited to performance situations such as giving a speech. Only a qualified professional can assess whether your fear is a clinical condition.

### Is glossophobia a mental illness?
Fear of public speaking on its own is very common and not a diagnosis. It can be part of social anxiety disorder when it is intense, long-lasting and gets in the way of daily life. A GP or psychologist can assess this.

### Can glossophobia be treated?
Research shows that psychological treatments reduce the fear of public speaking. The UK's NICE guideline recommends a specific form of CBT as the first treatment for adults with social anxiety disorder. What is right for you should be decided with a healthcare professional.

### Who should I talk to first?
Usually your GP or primary care provider. In the UK you can also self-refer to NHS talking therapies. In Sweden, contact a vårdcentral or call 1177. If you are in danger, call your local emergency number.

## Support resources

You do not have to manage this on your own. If the fear of speaking is affecting your life, these are good next steps:

- **UK:** your GP, or self-refer to NHS talking therapies. Read the [NHS page on social anxiety](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/social-anxiety/).
- **US:** a primary care provider or mental health professional. Read [NIMH's guide to social anxiety disorder](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/social-anxiety-disorder-more-than-just-shyness). Crisis: call or text 988.
- **Sweden:** a vårdcentral, or call 1177. Read [1177's page on social anxiety](https://www.1177.se/Stockholm/sjukdomar--besvar/psykiska-sjukdomar-och-besvar/angest/social-angest/) (in Swedish). Emergency: 112.
- **Anywhere:** in an emergency, call your local emergency number.

For background reading, see [what happens in your body and brain when you fear public speaking](/en/fear-of-public-speaking-body-and-brain) and [research-based ways to calm nerves before a talk](/en/calm-nerves-before-a-talk).
