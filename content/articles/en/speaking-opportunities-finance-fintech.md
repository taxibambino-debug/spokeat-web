# Speaking opportunities for finance and fintech experts

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

Finance and fintech people have something many audiences want: an inside view of how money, risk and regulation actually work. Students want to know what the job is like. Peers want to know what changed this quarter. Founders want to know how investors and banks think.

The problem is that finance speaking slots are scattered. Some sit inside university courses. Some belong to student finance societies. Others are breakfast seminars at chambers of commerce, or sessions at large conferences that may expect you to pay.

This page shows the main types of venue where finance and fintech experts speak, with real examples we checked on 16 November 2026. We do not promise that any of these organisers has a slot for you. We describe where opportunities typically come from and what each type expects.

## Key takeaways

- University courses and business schools invite finance practitioners, for example in accounting and finance modules or finance speaker series [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2).
- Student finance societies run guest lectures and employer sessions, and they plan term by term [[6]](#ref-6) [[7]](#ref-7).
- Chambers of commerce and professional networks run breakfast seminars and economic outlook sessions, often with bank or market speakers [[10]](#ref-10) [[11]](#ref-11).
- Large fintech conferences can be competitive and may involve costs for speakers. Read the terms before you apply [[14]](#ref-14) [[15]](#ref-15).
- Podcasts are an underused route. Some state that guests do not sponsor episodes [[16]](#ref-16).

## University courses and business schools

Finance courses lend themselves to practitioner input. A textbook explains how a payment flows. A practitioner explains what breaks.

**Examples we verified:**

- **Sheffield University Management School** lists past guest talks, including one on "Raising investment for startups" in an accounting and finance for managers module. Sessions usually last 30 to 40 minutes plus 15 to 20 minutes of questions [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Cambridge Judge Business School** runs a Master of Finance City Speaker Series with finance professionals as "subject-matter experts", followed by Q&A and dinner. It also invites Practitioner Insight Speakers in areas "such as marketing or finance" [[2]](#ref-2).
- **Hanken School of Economics** (Helsinki) invites alumni to "offer your expertise for a guest lecture for both Hanken students and other alumni" [[3]](#ref-3).
- **Lund University School of Economics and Management (LUSEM)** says partner companies can share expertise "through guest lectures" [[4]](#ref-4).
- **Stockholm School of Economics** runs tech talks through its Tech Initiative. Past speakers listed include a Mastercard SVP and Tink's Head of Data & Analytics [[5]](#ref-5).

**Course types to look for:** corporate finance, banking, payments, accounting, financial regulation, entrepreneurial finance, and information systems courses that cover financial technology.

## Student finance societies

Student finance societies exist to connect members with the industry. That makes them natural hosts for practitioners, though they often link talks to employer relations. Our guide on [speaking at student societies](/en/speak-at-student-societies) covers how they plan and whom to contact.

**Examples we verified:**

- **LINC, Lund University Finance Society**, which LUSEM describes as offering "inspiring guest lectures, practical analysis groups, workshops" and trips to network with companies and banks [[6]](#ref-6).
- **The Finance Society of F.E.St.**, the business association at Stockholm University, which says: "We also bring employers to campus for interactive sessions, panel discussions, and networking events." [[7]](#ref-7)
- **LundaEkonomerna**, the student union at LUSEM, whose Corporate Relations Committee has run lunch lectures. One featured a Grant Thornton data analytics specialist speaking about AI, digitalisation and auditing [[8]](#ref-8).

**What they usually want:** career insight, a clear explanation of a current topic, and time for questions. They rarely want a product demo.

## Chambers of commerce, associations and professional networks

Chambers and associations run regular programmes for members. Finance topics are common, especially economic outlooks and regulation. Our guide on [speaking at industry associations and chambers](/en/speak-at-industry-associations-and-chambers) explains how these programmes are put together.

**Examples we verified:**

- **AmCham Sweden** listed "Economic Outlook with Nasdaq" on 18 November 2026, a morning briefing on capital markets, regulation and Sweden's economic outlook [[10]](#ref-10).
- **British-Swedish Chamber of Commerce (BSCC)** runs "Financial Outlook" breakfasts with bank economists and listed a session called "All things Digital Assets - Trends & Developments" in March 2026 [[11]](#ref-11).
- **Swedish Chamber of Commerce for the UK** ran "Ecosystem Exchange: Fintech 2026" during UK Fintech Week, with masterclasses and panels featuring speakers from HSBC Innovation Banking, Revolut and Mastercard [[12]](#ref-12).
- **Sydsvenska Industri- och Handelskammaren** runs "Ekonomen", a network for people in finance departments with 6 to 8 meetings a year that include talks [[9]](#ref-9).
- **Nordic Blockchain Association** has hosted Stockholm meetups with "short presentations, networking opportunities, and plenty of time for Q&A", such as "Careers in Crypto: Wallet Security & Web3 Essentials" [[13]](#ref-13).

**A note on outlook sessions:** the outlook slots we saw were filled by bank or exchange speakers. If you are not a chief economist, pitch a narrower topic instead. For example, a compliance lead might offer a practical session on what a new rule means for member companies. That example is hypothetical.

## Conferences and podcasts

Large fintech conferences give visibility, but they are competitive and the terms vary. Read the small print.

- **Money20/20 Europe** had a call for content open from 2 to 27 November 2026 for its June 2027 event. It asks for "original ideas, fearless conversations and useful insights, not sales pitches". Selected applicants are offered a pass at €2,495 + VAT, and the submitting company covers its speakers' costs [[14]](#ref-14).
- **Open Banking Expo** says speaker selection is "highly competitive, with a limited number of sessions available". Its form also asks whether you would consider speaking as part of a broader commercial partnership if not selected [[15]](#ref-15).
- **Payments on Fire**, Glenbrook's payments podcast, has a guest inquiry form and states: "Payments on Fire is not sponsored by our guests." [[16]](#ref-16)

When a speaking slot is linked to a fee or a sponsorship package, you are buying visibility, not earning a speaking invitation. Our article on [paying to speak](/en/paying-to-speak) explains how to tell the difference.

## Topics that tend to work

Organisers in finance respond to topics that are specific, current and useful. These are illustrative examples, not guaranteed formats:

- How a regulation changes daily work for a bank, payment firm or crypto platform.
- What a payments or treasury team actually does all day, for students choosing a career.
- Lessons from a product launch, migration or fraud incident, without confidential details.
- How investors or lenders assess an early-stage company.
- How data and AI are used in audit, risk or credit, as in the LundaEkonomerna example [[8]](#ref-8).

**A practical point:** finance professionals often work under employer rules on public statements. Check your employer's policy before you agree to speak, and keep the talk educational rather than about investment decisions.

## How to pitch as a finance expert

1. **Match the audience.** Students want careers and concepts. Peers want what is new. Founders want how decisions are made.
2. **Name one topic.** "Payments" is too broad. "What happens between tap and settlement" is a talk.
3. **Show credibility briefly.** Your role, your years in the field and one concrete result.
4. **Keep it non-commercial.** Student and community organisers rarely accept sales talks.
5. **Follow up once.** If there is no reply, move on to the next organiser.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where can a fintech expert give a guest lecture?
Look at business schools and economics faculties with finance, payments or information systems courses. Several schools publish guest-speaker routes, such as Cambridge Judge's finance speaker series and Sheffield's guest speaker page [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2).

### Do finance conferences pay speakers?
Many do not, and some expect speakers to pay. Money20/20 Europe offers selected speakers a discounted pass at €2,495 + VAT and says the submitting company covers its speakers' costs [[14]](#ref-14). Always read the terms.

### How do I speak at a student finance society?
Contact the society's committee for events or corporate relations. LINC, for example, lists a business-enquiries address on its site, and societies such as F.E.St. bring employers to campus for sessions and panels [[17]](#ref-17) [[7]](#ref-7).

### Is a podcast a real speaking opportunity?
Yes. A podcast interview builds credibility much like a talk and is often easier to schedule. Payments on Fire has an open inquiry form and says guests do not sponsor episodes [[16]](#ref-16).
