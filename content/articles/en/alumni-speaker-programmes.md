# Alumni speaker programmes: how they work and how to get involved

> **Written by** [Ted Scheiman](/authors/ted-scheiman), banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. [How we research and fact-check](/about/editorial-policy)

Most universities have an alumni office. Fewer people know that many of these offices run, or quietly support, a programme for alumni who want to speak to students. It may be called a speaker series, a guest lecturer register or simply a line on the "get involved" page.

These programmes are one of the most reliable ways for a working professional to get in front of students. The university has already decided it wants alumni speakers. Your job is to make it easy for them to place you.

This guide explains the main types of alumni speaker programmes, how matching works, what alumni offices look for and how to sign up. All examples were checked on the universities' own pages on 16 November 2026.

## Key takeaways
- Alumni speaker programmes range from a simple invitation and an email address to structured speaker series with fixed dates.
- Most work by matching: you register your expertise, and the office or a teacher contacts you when a course or event fits.
- Alumni offices ask for a clear area of expertise and real-world experience, not academic credentials [[1]](#ref-1) [[2]](#ref-2).
- Expect to teach or share your career story, not to present your company [[3]](#ref-3).
- Registration does not guarantee a slot, and talks are usually unpaid [[3]](#ref-3).

## What an alumni speaker programme is

An alumni speaker programme is any arrangement through which a university invites its former students to speak to current students. In practice there are four common models.

**1. An open invitation with a contact address.** The simplest model. The alumni page says alumni can give guest lectures and gives an address. Stockholm University asks alumni to get in touch about the topic they would like to present and says it will try to match them with a suitable student audience [[1]](#ref-1). Hanken School of Economics in Finland invites alumni to offer guest lectures to students and other alumni [[4]](#ref-4).

**2. A register or sign-up form.** The alumni office keeps a list of willing speakers. KTH asks alumni to register their interest in guest lecturing through an online form, and organises the lectures together with KTH Career Service [[5]](#ref-5).

**3. A menu of formats.** Some offices list several ways to contribute. The University of Edinburgh Business School lists alumni panels, on-course speaking, career lounges and student visits, both in person and online [[2]](#ref-2).

**4. A structured speaker series.** Some business schools run named series with fixed rhythms. Cambridge Judge Business School runs an EMBA speaker series with talks once a term followed by a Friday speaker dinner with students, and a Master of Finance City Speaker Series with Q&A and dinner [[6]](#ref-6).

## How matching works

Behind most programmes is the same simple process:

1. **You register or send a short description.** Your field, role and what you could talk about.
2. **The office holds your details.** At Aalto, a proposal stays valid for one year unless you refresh it, and lecturers can view it during that time [[3]](#ref-3).
3. **A need comes up.** A teacher wants a practitioner for a module, or the office plans a panel or career event.
4. **Someone contacts you.** Aalto says its lecturers "will contact you when they find a topic suitable for their course" [[3]](#ref-3).
5. **You agree format and date.** Usually with the teacher or event organiser directly.

This has one big consequence: you are waiting for a match. If your description is vague, nobody will think of you when a need appears. If it names a clear topic and audience, it is easy to pick.

## What alumni offices look for

The pages are short, but they point in the same direction.

**A clear area of expertise.** Edinburgh asks alumni to contact the team "providing details of your area of expertise" [[2]](#ref-2). Cambridge Judge describes its Master of Finance speakers as subject-matter experts in applied finance [[6]](#ref-6).

**Real-world experience.** Stockholm University writes that guest lecturers who can share real-world experience are in high demand among students and academic advisors [[1]](#ref-1).

**Career stories as well as subject knowledge.** At KTH, alumni share their professional experiences and career journeys and help students understand the move from university to working life [[5]](#ref-5). Uppsala University's guidance for its staff says alumni can give students a concrete picture of their opportunities after graduation and improve the quality and relevance of programmes and courses [[8]](#ref-8).

**Content, not promotion.** Aalto asks guest lecturers to focus on course content rather than a company presentation [[3]](#ref-3).

**Any career stage.** Edinburgh welcomes alumni "from recent graduates to senior board members" [[2]](#ref-2). You do not need to be a chief executive.

## Formats you may be offered

- **Guest lecture in a course.** One session inside a scheduled course, tied to its content.
- **Alumni panel.** Several alumni discuss careers or a theme. A good first step if you have not spoken before.
- **Fireside chat.** A moderated conversation. In February 2025, the Uppsala University Alumni Network held one with an alumnus for students in economics, industrial engineering and business management, moderated by two faculty members [[9]](#ref-9).
- **Career lounge or career talk.** Edinburgh describes career lounges as a way to share your career journey and introduce potential future talent to your business and industry [[2]](#ref-2).
- **Real-life case.** At Aalto, students work on a short challenge from an expert and present their recommendations back [[3]](#ref-3).
- **Graduation speech.** Uppsala lists holding a speech at a graduation ceremony as one way to get involved [[7]](#ref-7).

## How to get involved: a step-by-step plan

1. **Find your university's alumni page.** Look for "get involved", "give back" or "volunteer". Note the format they ask for: a form, an email address or a register.
2. **Write a two-line expertise statement.** For example: "Head of payments at a Nordic bank. Can talk about instant payments, regulation and careers in payments."
3. **Offer two or three topics.** One subject talk linked to the programme you studied, one career talk, and optionally a panel theme.
4. **State your constraints.** City, whether you can speak online, and how often you are available.
5. **Contact the department as well.** Some universities accept both routes. Our guide to [speaking at your old university](/en/speak-at-your-old-university) explains how to approach a course directly.
6. **Refresh once a year.** Registrations can expire, as at Aalto [[3]](#ref-3). Send a short update at the start of each academic year.

A hypothetical example: a sustainability manager who studied engineering registers with her university's alumni office. She lists three topics: how a mid-sized manufacturer measures emissions, what the first two years in a sustainability role look like, and a panel on green careers. Four months later, a teacher in an environmental management course contacts her about a single session in the spring.

## If you are not an alumnus

You can still take part in some programmes. Aalto's Pool of Experts invites "alumni and other corporate representatives" to take part in teaching [[3]](#ref-3). KTH tells companies that they can offer guest lectures through the student union or by contacting the teacher of a specific programme [[10]](#ref-10). Student societies are another good route; see [how to speak at student societies](/en/speak-at-student-societies).

## Frequently asked questions

### Are alumni speakers paid?
Usually not. Aalto University School of Business states that it does not pay a fee to external participants [[3]](#ref-3), and none of the other alumni pages we checked mention payment. Ask about travel expenses if you need to travel.

### How long does it take to get matched?
It depends on when relevant courses run. Programmes match speakers to courses and events as needs come up, and Aalto notes it may not be able to take up every proposal [[3]](#ref-3). A follow-up to the alumni office or a direct approach to a teacher can speed things up.

### Can I speak online through an alumni programme?
Some programmes allow it. The University of Edinburgh Business School says its alumni opportunities run both in person and online [[2]](#ref-2). Ask the alumni office if it is not stated.

### Do I need to have spoken before?
No. Alumni pages ask for expertise and experience, not speaking credentials. Panels and fireside chats are a gentle way to start.

### Can I join more than one programme?
Yes, if you studied at more than one university. Register at each and tailor your topics to each programme.
