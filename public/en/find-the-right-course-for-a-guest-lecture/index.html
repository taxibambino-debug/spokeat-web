How to find the right university course for a guest lecture

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

A common guest lecture pitch goes like this. The professional picks a famous university, writes a general offer, and hopes someone forwards it to the right person. Often nobody does.

Stronger pitches start from the other end. They begin with one course, one teacher and one learning outcome the speaker can genuinely help with.

This guide shows you how to find that course. You will learn where course information is published, which fields on a course page matter, and how to judge fit with a worked example. If you want Spoke At to do the searching, there is a link at the end.

Key takeaways

  • Look for a course, not a university. The teacher in charge of a course is usually the person who invites guests.
  • Course catalogues are public. They show the level, timing, learning outcomes, teaching format and assessment.
  • Learning outcomes are the most useful field. Your lecture should clearly support at least one.
  • Check when the course runs before you write. A perfect fit in the wrong term is not a fit.
  • Skip discontinued courses and courses with no teaching sessions where a guest could fit.

Why the course matters more than the university

Guest lectures are usually arranged at course level. Aalto University School of Business notes that guest lecturers are often invited by the teacher in charge of a course, and asks guests to agree the content in advance so it meets the course's learning objectives [2]. RMIT Europe connects online guest lecturers with a course coordinator in their field [8].

Students also respond better to guests who fit. In a survey of 114 students, those who had experienced industry guest speakers valued them most when the speaker currently worked in a field related to the course content [1]. In another survey of 107 communication students, 72.5% wanted a topic closely tied to the course. Even higher shares wanted career advice (88.3%) and the speaker's professional journey (85.4%) [3]. Both studies come from single US institutions, so read them as indications. Still, the pattern is useful: a good guest lecture links the course topic to real careers.

For a broader overview of how universities book guests, read how to become a guest lecturer at a university.

Where to find course information

Most universities publish a searchable course catalogue. Names vary: course catalogue, module directory, programme and course handbook, or study guide. The University of Edinburgh, for example, publishes a page for each course with its level, credits, semester, course organiser, learning outcomes, teaching hours and assessment [4].

Useful ways in:

  • Search by keyword. Use the words a teacher would use, not marketing terms. "Payment systems" and "financial regulation" will find more than "fintech disruption".
  • Browse by programme. Master's programmes often list their courses on one page. Start with programmes close to your field.
  • Check the school or department. Business schools, engineering faculties and law schools each have their own course lists.
  • Look for guest lecture pages. Some schools describe how they use guests. Sheffield University Management School lists past guest topics by module, such as raising investment for startups in an accounting and finance module for managers [7].

How to read a course page

Not every field matters equally. These are the ones to check, and what each tells you.

Field What to look for Why it matters
Title and summary The real focus of the course Tells you whether your topic is core or marginal
Level Undergraduate or postgraduate Sets the depth and examples you can use
When it runs Semester, term or study period Tells you when to get in touch
Learning outcomes What students should be able to do after the course Your lecture must support at least one
Teaching methods Lectures, seminars, case work, hours Shows whether there are sessions a guest could join
Assessment Essays, exams, group work Shows how your input could help students later
Contact Course organiser, coordinator or module leader The person you write to

Two warning signs are worth checking early. First, is the course still running? KTH's page for one finance course, for example, states that the course has been discontinued and lists no upcoming course rounds [6]. Second, which year is the page for? Catalogues are published per academic year, so make sure you are reading the current or next year's version.

Worked example: a fintech policy course

Here is how the process looks with a real course page. The professional in this example is hypothetical.

The professional. For example, a head of payments compliance at a European payments company. They have spent years working with regulators on new payment products.

The course. The University of Edinburgh's FinTech Infrastructures and Policy (CMSE11645) is a postgraduate course at SCQF level 11, worth 20 SCQF credits (10 ECTS). It is part of the Finance, Technology and Policy MSc [4].

Step 1: Read the summary. The course examines the economic, social and political effects of new financial technologies and the role of policy and regulation. It focuses on the infrastructures behind financial markets and fintech products, rather than on individual technologies [4]. That is a close match for someone who works on regulated payment infrastructure.

Step 2: Match the learning outcomes. One learning outcome is to understand the mandates of financial regulators and the trade-offs they face, particularly regarding fintech. Another is to apply academic concepts to assess how new financial products and technologies affect markets, institutions and society [4]. A real case about how a regulatory requirement shaped a payment product supports both.

Step 3: Check the teaching format. The course has 20 lecture hours and 4 seminar or tutorial hours. Seminars use practical case studies [4]. A case-based guest session fits that format better than a slide-heavy talk.

Step 4: Look at the assessment. Students write an individual essay (80%) and give a group presentation (20%). There is no exam [4]. A concrete case could give students material for their essays. That is a useful point to mention in your email.

Step 5: Check the timing. The course runs in Semester 1 of 2026/27. At Edinburgh, Semester 1 runs from 21 September to 21 December 2026, and the second teaching block ends on 4 December [5]. By mid-November that course round is almost over. The right move is to check whether the course appears in the 2027/28 catalogue and get in touch well before the next Semester 1.

Step 6: Note what the page does not say. The course page does not mention guest speakers [4]. That does not mean guests are unwelcome, but it does mean you are offering something, not answering a call. Keep the email short and specific, and make it easy to say yes.

The result. One course, one named role (the course organiser), two learning outcomes the lecture supports, a format that suits the course, and a clear time to get in touch. That is a pitch worth writing. Our guest lecture email templates show how to turn it into a short email.

A quick fit checklist

Score each course from 0 to 2 on each line. Courses scoring 8 or more are worth a pitch. This scale is our own working tool, not a research-based measure.

  • Topic: Is your topic central to the course summary?
  • Learning outcomes: Can you name the outcome your lecture supports?
  • Level: Can you pitch your examples at the course's level?
  • Format: Are there lectures, seminars or case sessions a guest could join?
  • Timing: Is there enough time before the course starts to plan you in?
  • Contact: Is the responsible person named or easy to identify?

Common mistakes when choosing a course

  • Choosing by brand. A well-known university with no matching course is a worse target than a smaller one with a perfect course.
  • Reading only the title. Course titles can mislead. The learning outcomes tell you what is actually taught.
  • Ignoring the level. First-year undergraduates and experienced MBA students need very different lectures.
  • Pitching a course that has started. Check the dates. Our guide to the academic calendar for guest lecturers explains when to get in touch.
  • Using old catalogue pages. Always check the year on the page.

Frequently asked questions

Where can I find university course catalogues?

Most universities publish them on their own websites, often under "courses", "modules" or "study". Search the university's site for the course catalogue or module directory, then filter by school or programme.

What are learning outcomes and why do they matter?

Learning outcomes describe what students should be able to do after the course. Teachers plan every session around them, and Aalto University School of Business asks guests to agree content so it meets these objectives [2]. Naming the outcome you support shows the teacher you have read the course.

Should I pitch undergraduate or postgraduate courses?

Pick the level where your experience fits best. Postgraduate and professional courses often suit specialist case material, while undergraduate courses may value career insight and broad context. The course page tells you which level you are reading.

How many courses should I pitch at once?

Quality beats volume. A few well-matched courses, each with a specific email, are more likely to get a reply than many general messages.

What if the course page does not name a contact?

Look for the department's teaching or programme administration page, or the programme director. Avoid general university inboxes if you can.
