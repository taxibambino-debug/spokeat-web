How to calm nerves before a talk: techniques backed by research

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

You have a talk in an hour and your stomach is in knots. Search for advice and you will find hundreds of tips, from breathing tricks to "power poses". Some have decent evidence behind them. Some do not.

This guide covers techniques that have been tested in research, what each study actually found, and where the evidence stops. It is written for ordinary pre-talk nerves. It is not treatment for an anxiety disorder, and it ends with when to seek professional help.

If you want to understand why your body reacts as it does, start with what happens in your body and brain when you fear public speaking.

Key takeaways

  • Trying to "calm down" is not the only option. In one set of experiments, telling yourself "I am excited" led to better-rated speeches than "I am calm".
  • Slow, structured breathing has modest evidence for lowering stress in general, though not specifically for speaking.
  • Your nerves are less visible than they feel. Knowing this can reduce their grip.
  • Repeated, gradual practice is the approach with the strongest evidence for reducing the fear of public speaking over time.
  • "Power posing" is contested. Larger studies did not find the hormone effects first reported.

Technique 1: Call it excitement, not anxiety

What the research found. In experiments by Alison Wood Brooks at Harvard Business School, 140 participants prepared a persuasive speech that was videotaped. Before speaking, they said either "I am excited" or "I am calm". The "excited" group gave longer speeches and independent raters judged them more persuasive, competent and relaxed [1]. In a related karaoke task, the "excited" group scored 80% on the game's accuracy measure, compared with 69% for calm, angry or sad statements and 53% for "anxious" [1].

Why it may work. Excitement and anxiety are both high-arousal states. Brooks suggests that relabelling the arousal is easier than trying to remove it [1].

Limits. Participants were students and local community members. The source we checked is a press release from the American Psychological Association that reports few numbers for the speech study. This is one research group, not a body of independent replications.

How to try it. Before you go on, notice the racing heart and say to yourself, "I'm excited about this."

Technique 2: Slow your breathing

What the research found. A 2023 meta-analysis in Scientific Reports pooled 12 randomised controlled trials with 785 adults. Breathwork "was associated with lower levels of stress than control conditions", with a small-to-medium effect (g = −0.35) [2].

Limits. The authors rated most included studies at moderate risk of bias, and results varied between studies [2]. None of this is specific to public speaking. It tells you breathing practices may help stress in general.

How to try it. One simple option is to breathe in through your nose and breathe out slowly, a little longer than the in-breath. This is an example, not a specific protocol from the meta-analysis. Practise it on calm days too, so it is familiar when you need it. Stop if it makes you dizzy or feel worse.

Technique 3: Remember the audience sees less than you feel

What the research found. Research on the "illusion of transparency" shows that speakers overestimate how visible their nervousness is. A peer-reviewed replication summarises the original 2003 findings by Savitsky and Gilovich: the effects of this illusion were reduced by "simply assuring speakers" that their anxiety was less visible than they believed [3].

Limits. These are lab studies with short, impromptu speeches. Some nervous signs, such as a shaky voice, can be visible. The point is that they are usually less obvious than you think.

How to try it. Remind yourself before you start: "They can't see most of what I feel."

Technique 4: Point your attention outwards

What the research found. In an experiment with 120 participants giving a speech, simply watching yourself on a live screen raised cortisol in men about as much as speaking to an evaluative panel [4]. Self-focus, in other words, seemed to act like being judged.

Limits. The effect appeared in men only, with no effects found for women, and it is one study [4]. It does not prove that shifting attention outwards lowers anxiety. It suggests that intense self-monitoring is not neutral.

How to try it. Avoid watching yourself in a mirror or on a self-view video just before you speak. During the talk, focus on one friendly face, on your message, or on what the audience needs.

Technique 5: Practise in steps, and drop the "safety behaviours"

What the research found. A 2019 meta-analysis of 30 randomised controlled trials (1,355 participants) found that psychological interventions reduced the fear of public speaking, with a moderate-to-large effect (Hedges' g = 0.74). Effects did not differ across theoretical frameworks, and technology-assisted formats appeared about as effective as face-to-face ones [5].

Sweden's public health portal 1177 gives practical self-care advice that fits this: do the things you need to do even when they feel hard, stop "safety behaviours" such as avoiding eye contact or scripting every line in advance, and practise in easier situations before gradually increasing the difficulty [6].

Limits. The meta-analysis covers structured programmes, often led or guided by professionals. The authors noted "some indications of publication bias", and self-reported improvements were larger than physiological or behavioural ones [5]. Practising on your own is not the same as treatment.

How to try it. Start with a short slot for a friendly group, such as a first talk at a meetup, before you aim for a bigger stage. Each time, drop one safety behaviour.

Technique 6: Look after the basics

1177 also recommends exercise, sleep, relaxation and breathing exercises, regular meals, avoiding alcohol and drugs, and cutting down on nicotine and caffeine as part of general self-care for social anxiety [6]. On the day of a talk, that means a proper meal, a sensible amount of coffee, and no "drink for courage".

What about power posing?

You may have heard that standing like a superhero for two minutes raises testosterone and lowers cortisol. That claim came from a small 2010 study with 42 participants. A larger replication with 200 people found a small effect on self-reported feelings of power, but "failed to confirm an effect of power posing on testosterone, cortisol, and financial risk taking" [7].

The debate continued. A 2019 commentary in Meta-Psychology argued that in the studies with a neutral comparison, the effects were driven mainly by a contractive (hunched) pose making people feel worse, not by an expansive pose making them feel better [8].

Bottom line: standing upright will not hurt, but do not expect it to change your hormones.

The limits of self-help

The techniques above are for ordinary nerves. They are not a cure for an anxiety disorder, and they may not be enough if your fear is severe.

The NHS says it is a good idea to see a GP if you think you have social anxiety, and that in the UK you can refer yourself directly to an NHS talking therapies service [9]. The US National Institute of Mental Health suggests talking to a health care provider such as a primary care doctor, psychiatrist, psychologist or clinical social worker [10].

Some people ask about medication for performance nerves. That is a medical decision to make with a doctor, not something to try on your own.

Seek help from your GP or healthcare provider if your nerves:

  • are persistent or getting worse;
  • make you avoid opportunities you want or need;
  • come with panic attacks;
  • lead you to use alcohol or other substances to cope.

If you are in immediate danger or thinking about suicide, call your local emergency number now. In the US, NIMH lists the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline [10]. For more detail, read when the fear of speaking needs professional help.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to calm nerves before a presentation?

There is no guaranteed quick fix. Two options with some research support are relabelling your arousal as excitement and slow breathing. Both have limits: the excitement study is from one research group, and the breathing evidence is about stress in general.

Should I tell myself to calm down?

Not necessarily. In Brooks's experiments, people who said "I am excited" before a speech were rated more persuasive and competent than those who said "I am calm". The findings come from a small number of experiments.

Does power posing work before a talk?

The evidence is contested. A larger replication found no effect on testosterone or cortisol, though people reported feeling slightly more powerful. Standing upright is fine, but do not rely on it to change your body chemistry.

When are presentation nerves a reason to see a doctor?

When they are persistent or severe, cause panic attacks, make you avoid important opportunities, or lead you to use alcohol or other substances to cope. Your GP or healthcare provider is the right first contact.

Practise at a meetup or society

Repeated practice in front of real, friendly audiences is one of the best-supported ways to make speaking feel more manageable. A student society or a local meetup is a good place to start.
