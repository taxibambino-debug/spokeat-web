Guest lecture email: how to approach a course coordinator (with templates)

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

You have found a university course that fits your expertise. Now you need to write to the person who runs it. That one email decides whether you get a reply, and most first attempts are too long, too vague or about the speaker rather than the students.

This guide gives you two templates you can adapt: a formal one and a warm, direct one. It also covers what to leave out, when to send and which language to write in. Spoke At can draft this email for you in the organiser's language, but everything below works on its own.

Key takeaways

  • Write to the person responsible for the course, by name, and name the course and its code.
  • Offer a topic area linked to the syllabus, not a finished talk. Stay under 150 words.
  • End with one clear question the coordinator can answer in a minute.
  • Leave out attachments, long bios, sales language and fee negotiations.
  • Send well before the course starts, in the language the course is taught in.

Why a short, direct email works

Two research findings are worth knowing before you write.

First, people tend to underestimate how often others say yes. In studies by Francis Flynn and Vanessa Lake, people asking for help underestimated by as much as 50% the likelihood that others would agree to a direct request [1]. In their role-play studies, askers also believed a hint would work better than a direct question, while the potential helpers said they were more likely to help when asked directly [2]. The practical lesson: ask plainly for what you want.

Second, email requests are easy to ignore. In an experiment by Mahdi Roghanizad and Vanessa Bohns, 45 students each asked 10 strangers to fill in a short survey, either in person or by email. Both groups expected about 5 in 10 to agree. Email requests succeeded about 10% of the time, compared with about 70% face to face [3]. The authors describe the findings as exploratory and based on students, so do not treat these numbers as a forecast for a coordinator's inbox. What the study does suggest is that the reader cannot see you, so the first lines of your email have to show who you are and why you are writing to them specifically.

Who to write to

Write to the person responsible for the course. Universities use different titles: course coordinator, course leader, module leader or course director. In Sweden the role is usually called kursansvarig. You will normally find the name on the course page or in the syllabus. Our guide on how to find the right course for a guest lecture shows where to look.

Some universities also run central routes for alumni. Stockholm University asks alumni with an idea to email its alumni office and says that guest lecturers who can share real-world experience are in high demand [4]. KTH collects alumni interest in guest lecturing through a form, run together with its career service [5]. If you studied at the university, you can use such a route as well as writing to a coordinator about a specific course.

Do not write to several teachers on the same course at once, and do not copy the department inbox. It reads as a mass mailing and puts the coordinator in an awkward position.

What the coordinator needs from your email

A coordinator reading your email wants answers to five questions: who are you, why this course, what would students get, when, and what are you asking for? Keep each answer to a sentence or two.

Element What to write Keep it to
Subject line Course code or name plus "guest lecture" Under 10 words
Who you are Role, organisation, the area you work in 1 sentence
Why this course A syllabus topic or module you have read about 1 sentence
What you offer A topic area and a rough length 1 to 2 sentences
Credibility One relevant, true fact (experience, teaching, a project) 1 sentence
The ask One question about fit and timing 1 sentence

On length of the session itself, one useful reference point: the University of Sheffield's management school says its guest sessions usually last around 30 to 40 minutes plus 15 to 20 minutes of questions, and that students want insight into the speaker's current role, organisation and career so far [6]. Formats vary between universities, so offer a length and say you can adapt.

Template 1: formal

Use this for a first contact with a professor or senior academic, for courses at a traditional university, or when the course page is written in a formal tone.

Subject: Guest lecture offer for [course code] [course name]

Dear [Title] [Surname],

I am [role] at [organisation], where I work on [specific area]. I have read the course description for [course code] [course name] and saw that [module, week or theme] covers [syllabus topic].

I would be glad to give a guest lecture of around [30 to 45] minutes on [topic area], drawing on my experience of [one concrete, true example]. I can adapt the session to the course's learning outcomes and the students' level.

Would a session like this fit into [term or period, e.g. spring 2027]? If so, I am happy to suggest dates or have a short call.

Kind regards, [Full name] [Role, organisation] [Profile link] | [Phone]

Template 2: warm and direct

Use this for lecturers in your own field, business school programmes, or courses where the staff page and course page are informal. It is shorter and reads like one professional writing to another.

Subject: Guest session on [topic] for [course name]?

Hi [First name],

I'm [name], [role] at [organisation]. Your course [course name] covers [syllabus topic], which is what I work on day to day.

Would a 30-minute guest session with real examples from [industry] be useful for your students this [term]? I'll fit it around your plan and the course goals.

If it's of interest, I can send two or three possible angles.

Best, [Name] [Profile link]

How to fill in the blanks

The template only works if the brackets are specific. Compare these two hypothetical opening lines from a head of payments:

  • Weak: "I have many years of experience in finance and would love to share my insights with your students."
  • Better: "I lead the payments team at [organisation], and your module on financial infrastructure in week 5 covers card schemes, which is what my team works on."

The second version proves you read the course page. It also gives the coordinator a place in the course to put you.

Only use facts that are true and that you can back up. If you have never given a guest lecture, do not imply that you have. Relevant work experience is enough.

What to leave out

Leave out Why
Attachments (CV, slides, brochures) Unrequested files look like marketing and may be filtered. Offer to send more if useful.
A long bio One relevant fact is more convincing than a paragraph. Link to a profile instead.
A full talk outline The coordinator decides how a session fits the course. Offer a topic area and listen.
Product or sales content A guest lecture is teaching, not a pitch. A whiff of sales usually ends the conversation.
Fee negotiation Do not open with money. If you are happy to do it unpaid, one short phrase is enough.
Several asks One question per email. "Would this fit in spring?" is easy to answer.
Flattery and filler "I'm a huge fan of your work" adds words, not trust.
Pressure No deadlines or "I only have a few slots". You are asking for their time.

When to send

Courses are planned before they start. Stockholm University's spring 2027 term runs from 18 January to 6 June 2027 and is split into shorter periods starting on 18 January, 22 February, 29 March and 3 May [7]. A course that starts next week rarely has room for a new guest. A course that starts in a later period might. As a rule of thumb, write a term ahead, or early in the period before the course runs. Our guide to the academic calendar for guest lecturers goes into more detail.

Avoid the first week of a term and exam weeks, when coordinators are busiest.

Write in the coordinator's language

If the course is taught in Swedish, write in Swedish. If it is taught in English, as many master's programmes are, English is fine. Look at how the course page and staff pages address readers, and match that tone.

Our Swedish version of this guide has the same two templates written for Swedish universities.

After you send

Wait about a week. If you have not heard back, send one short follow-up to the same person, then stop. Do not write to their colleagues or the department about the same course. Our guide on what to do when an organiser does not reply explains why and what to try instead.

If you are new to this, start with how to become a guest lecturer at a university for the bigger picture.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a guest lecture email be?

Aim for 60 to 150 words. That is enough to say who you are, why this course and what you offer. A coordinator should be able to read it on a phone and answer with one line.

Should I attach my CV or slides?

No. Unrequested attachments look like marketing and add work for the reader. Put a link to a professional profile in your signature and offer to send more if the coordinator wants it.

Who should I email, the professor or the department?

Email the person responsible for the specific course, as named on the course page or syllabus. Do not copy the department inbox or other teachers. If the coordinator says someone else plans guest sessions, write to that person and mention who referred you.

Should I mention payment in the first email?

Do not negotiate fees in the first email. If you are happy to speak without a fee, a short phrase is enough. If you need a fee, raise it once the coordinator shows interest.
