How to get invited onto podcasts as a guest expert

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

A podcast interview is one of the easiest speaking slots to get. There is no stage, no travel and often no slides. The host does much of the work by asking questions. For many professionals it is a good place to practise explaining their field out loud.

The hard part is getting the invitation. Most hosts receive more pitches than they can use, and most of those pitches are generic. This guide explains how hosts choose guests, how to find the right shows in your niche, what a useful pitch contains, how to prepare, and how to get value from the episode after it is published.

Key takeaways

  • Podcast listening is large and stable: in the US, 58% of people aged 12+ consumed a podcast in the past month in early 2026 [1]. In Sweden, 32% listen at least weekly [3].
  • Hosts pick guests for fit with their audience, not for job titles. Pitch the show you actually listen to.
  • Small, niche shows are easier to get onto and often reach exactly the right people.
  • A good pitch is short, specific and shows you know the format.
  • Use a microphone and a quiet room. Audio quality is part of being a good guest.

Why podcasts are worth your time

The audience is there. The Infinite Dial 2026, a US survey by Edison Research with 2,050 respondents aged 12 and over, found that 58% had consumed a podcast in the last month and 45% in the last week, both record highs. Among 35 to 54 year olds, the figure for the last month was 68% [1]. That age group overlaps with the decision makers many experts want to reach.

In Sweden, the Media Barometer 2025 from Nordicom at the University of Gothenburg found that 29% listened to podcasts or radio on demand on an average day [2]. The Poddindex annual report for 2025 cites ORVESTO Konsument data showing that 32% of Swedes listen to podcasts at least weekly, a share that has been unchanged since 2022. The average listener was 42 years old [3].

The numbers say podcasts are a mainstream medium. They do not say that any single episode will bring you clients or invitations. Treat a podcast appearance like a guest lecture: a chance to be useful to a specific audience, and something you can point to later.

How hosts choose guests

Spotify's guidance for creators tells hosts to "get clear on what makes a good fit for your show" before they reach out, and to judge guests by audience fit and whether they add something regular episodes do not [4]. It also tells hosts to start with their own network, because those people are "easier to approach, more likely to say yes" [4].

That has two consequences for you:

  1. Fit beats fame. A host is not looking for the most senior person available. They are looking for someone whose knowledge suits their listeners.
  2. Warm contacts win. If you know the host, have been a listener for a while, or share a network, say so. It is the main thing that separates you from cold pitches.

Some shows publish exactly what they want. Payments on Fire, a payments podcast from Glenbrook Partners, has a guest inquiry form that asks "What message do you have to share that would be of interest to our listeners?" and states that "Payments on Fire is not sponsored by our guests" [5]. Many shows have no form at all. Then the host or producer decides from email or direct messages.

Be careful with shows that charge guests to appear. That is advertising, not an invitation. Our guide to paying to speak explains how to judge it.

Finding the right shows

Start small and specific. A show about payments compliance with a few hundred dedicated listeners can be worth more to a payments expert than a general business show with a large audience.

Search the podcast directories by category. Apple Podcasts groups shows by category. The Swedish storefront, for example, has an Entrepreneurship category that lists popular and new shows, mixing Swedish and English titles [6]. Look at the shows in your field, then listen to two or three recent episodes of each before you pitch.

Look at your industry's own media. Associations, trade publications and companies in your sector often run podcasts. Our guide to speaking opportunities in finance and fintech has a section on podcasts in that field, and the same method works in others.

Check where your peers have been guests. If someone in a similar role appeared on a show last month, that show already uses guests like you.

Note the details before you pitch:

Check Why it matters
Does the show use guests at all? Some are host-only or co-host formats
Episode length and style A 20-minute news show needs short answers; a 90-minute interview does not
Recent guests and topics Avoid pitching something they covered last month
Language Swedish or English, and whether you are comfortable in it
Contact route A form, a producer, or the host directly

What a good pitch contains

Buzzsprout, a podcast hosting company, tells hosts that "taking the time to write custom emails goes a long way toward getting a response", and that an outreach email should "zero in on the one thing you want" [7]. The same rule applies in reverse when you pitch a host.

A useful pitch has:

  1. A subject line with the topic, not "Guest request".
  2. One sentence showing you know the show, ideally naming a recent episode and what you took from it.
  3. The idea: one topic, framed for their listeners, with two or three angles you could cover.
  4. Why you: one or two lines on your role and what you have seen first-hand.
  5. Proof you can talk: a link to a previous interview, talk or recording, if you have one.
  6. One clear ask: "Would this fit an episode this spring?"

Keep it under 200 words. Do not attach a long biography. Make sure your public profile backs up what you say, because hosts will look you up. Our article on what organisers check in your speaker profile covers that. For wording that also works for podcasts, see our email templates for approaching organisers.

If you get no reply, one polite follow-up after a week or two is fine. After that, move on.

Preparing for the recording

A guide for researchers from the Centre for Society and Mental Health at King's College London gives practical advice that applies to any guest [8]:

  • Research the show and the interviewer, and ask for a broad list of topics they want to cover.
  • Find out how much work is involved, including any pre-call.
  • "Always use a microphone for the recording, never the laptop speaker."
  • Choose a quiet place. Open-plan offices and cafés are "not ideal".
  • "Try not to give overly long answers."
  • "Avoid technical terms and acronyms that the audience (and the interviewer) may not understand."

Two more practical points:

  • Know the format. Ask whether the episode is audio only or also filmed, how long it runs, and whether it is edited or published as recorded.
  • Prepare two or three points and an example for each. You are not giving a speech. You are making sure that, whatever the questions, the useful parts of your knowledge come out.

Do not name or quote clients, colleagues or employers without permission. Apple's content rules for podcast creators require that shows only include content they have the rights to use, and that metadata accurately represents the episode [9]. Help the host stay within those rules.

After the episode: reuse it

Spotify advises hosts to give guests a simple press kit with suggested copy, the episode link, cover art and a quote [4]. If the host does not send one, ask what they would like you to share. The King's College guide also suggests confirming how the episode will be promoted and adding it to your profile and CV [8].

Ways to reuse an appearance:

  • Share the episode with one or two sentences on the main point, not just "I was on a podcast".
  • Turn the best answer into a short written post.
  • Add the episode to your speaker profile as proof you can explain your topic.
  • Use it as a reference in your next pitch to an organiser or course coordinator.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get on podcasts as a guest with no experience?

Start with small, niche shows in your field, where hosts need knowledgeable guests and receive fewer pitches. Listen to recent episodes, pitch one specific topic for their audience and keep the email short. One recorded episode then becomes proof for the next pitch.

Should I pay to be a guest on a podcast?

A paid guest slot is advertising. It can be a marketing decision, but it is not an invitation earned on merit. Some shows state openly that guests do not pay, such as Payments on Fire.

What equipment do I need to be a podcast guest?

A separate microphone and a quiet room. A guide from King's College London advises always using a microphone rather than recording through the laptop, and avoiding open-plan offices and cafés. Ask the host whether they also film the conversation.

How long should a podcast pitch email be?

Short. Name the show and a recent episode, propose one topic with two or three angles, add a line on why you, and end with one clear question. Under 200 words is a good limit.
