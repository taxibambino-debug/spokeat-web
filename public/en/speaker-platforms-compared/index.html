Speaker platforms compared: directories, bureaus, Sessionize and more

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

Disclosure: this article is published by Spoke At, which is one of the types of service compared below. We have tried to describe every option fairly, using only what each provider says on its own pages, and we mark our own service clearly.

"Speaker platform" can mean very different things. Some tools exist to help conference organisers run a call for speakers. Some are directories where speakers publish a profile and hope to be found. Speaker bureaus represent paid speakers and sell them to event organisers. A newer category researches opportunities and helps you contact organisers directly.

Each type suits a different speaker at a different stage. This guide explains how each one works, who it suits and how it charges, with prices as published on 17 November 2026.

Key takeaways

  • There are four main types: call-for-speakers tools, speaker directories, speaker bureaus, and research-and-outreach services.
  • Call-for-speakers tools are where organisers collect talk proposals. You apply to one event at a time.
  • Directories let you publish a profile. Some charge an annual subscription for more features.
  • Speaker bureaus focus on paid speaking and are usually paid by commission from the speaker's fee.
  • Outreach services help you approach organisers who do not run open calls, such as university courses and associations.

1. Call-for-speakers tools

A call for speakers (or call for papers, CFP) is how many conferences and meetups choose talks. The organiser opens a form, speakers submit a title and abstract, and a committee selects.

Sessionize is one such tool. It describes itself as "the smart way to do Call for Papers, Schedule and Speaker Management" [1]. On its homepage it reports 323,000 speakers who have used it to apply, 89,000 public speaker profiles and 12,400 events [1]. Its features page lists benefits for speakers, including centralised management of talks for all events, getting found in a speakers directory, and discovering new events to speak at [7].

Its pricing page is aimed at organisers. Community events are free, but that tier is "not valid for internal, private, promotional, marketing, profit and commercial events". A professional event costs USD 499 plus tax for one event occurrence [2].

Who it suits: speakers targeting conferences and meetups that run open calls. The work is in finding the calls and writing a strong proposal for each one. Our guide to calls for speakers covers both.

Limits: you only reach events that use a public call. Many university courses, associations and company events never run one.

2. Speaker directories and marketplaces

A directory lets you publish a speaker profile so that organisers can find you. Some also list opportunities.

SpeakerHub publishes three plans for speakers. The free plan lets you publish a speaker page and access "a few free speaking opportunities". Silver costs USD 149.99 a year and includes "all FREE speaking opportunities". Gold costs USD 349.90 a year and includes "all FREE & PAID speaking opportunities". All three state "no commission or transaction fees" [3].

Who it suits: speakers who want a public profile that organisers can browse, and a feed of opportunities that others have listed.

Limits: a profile works when organisers search the directory. It is a passive channel. Your profile still needs to show the evidence organisers look for, which we cover in what organisers check in a speaker profile.

3. Speaker bureaus

A speaker bureau, or agency, represents speakers and sells their talks to clients such as companies and associations. The client is usually the event organiser, and the product is a paid speaking engagement.

How bureaus are paid, according to their own pages:

  • SPEAKING.com says it takes a commission from the speaker's fee and that there are no additional costs to the client for booking through it [4].
  • Leading Authorities says bureaus are compensated by the speaker as part of the agreed fee, and that speakers typically set their fees whether booked directly or through a bureau. It lists services such as coordinating contracts and travel logistics [6].
  • An episode of SpeakerFlow's podcast on how bureaus work says most bureaus charge a 25% commission, and that a couple of bureaus will not book a speaker whose fee is under USD 10,000 [8].

SPEAKING.com also gives a sense of the market it serves: it says keynote fees generally begin around USD 7,500, with an average national keynote fee of USD 10,000 [4]. It explains that some speakers are represented exclusively by one agency, and others work non-exclusively with many agencies [4].

In Sweden, Talarforum describes itself as an independent speaker agency ("oberoende talarförmedling") with sites for Sweden, Finland, Norway and Denmark. It says it is always looking for promising new speakers, moderators and entertainers to work with [5] [9].

Who it suits: speakers who already charge fees and have a track record that a bureau can sell.

Limits: bureaus earn from paid bookings, so their model fits established paid speakers. If you are building credibility through unpaid talks, a bureau is usually a later step. Our guide on finding speaking engagements without an agency covers the routes before that.

4. Research and outreach services (including ours)

Many good speaking slots never appear on a platform: a guest lecture in a specific university course, a talk at a student society, a session at an industry association. To get them you have to find the right person and write to them.

Spoke At is our service, and it works in this category. It researches opportunities such as university courses, business schools, student societies, associations, meetups, conferences and podcasts; identifies the right contact, for example the course coordinator; and drafts a personal outreach email in the organiser's language, which it can send for you. It sends at most two pitches per organiser per month and one polite follow-up. Search is free, users buy outreach packs, and there is a "no replies, no charge" guarantee. It does not book paid gigs and does not write talks or slides.

Who it suits: professionals who want visibility, credibility and practice through talks that are rarely advertised.

Limits: it does not sell you to clients the way a bureau does, and it does not replace a strong talk. You still need to prepare and deliver.

How to choose

Ask what you want next, not which platform is "best".

If you want to… Start with
Apply to tech and industry conferences with open calls A call-for-speakers tool, plus a list of open calls
Be findable by organisers who browse profiles A speaker directory
Get paid keynotes and already have a track record A speaker bureau
Get guest lectures and talks that are not advertised Direct outreach, by yourself or with a research service

Many speakers combine them. A public profile helps when an organiser checks you after a pitch, and open calls run alongside direct outreach.

Whichever route you take, watch for costs. Some events charge speakers, which is a separate issue from platform fees. Read our guide on paying to speak before you pay for any slot.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a speaker bureau and a speaker directory?

A bureau represents speakers and sells paid engagements to clients, usually earning a commission from the speaker's fee. A directory lets speakers publish a profile that organisers can browse, and may charge a subscription. Bureaus are active sales channels; directories are passive.

Is Sessionize free for speakers?

Sessionize's pricing page lists prices for organisers: free for community events and USD 499 plus tax per professional event. The pages we checked on 17 November 2026 did not state a price for speakers. Check Sessionize's own site for current terms.

How much does SpeakerHub cost?

On 17 November 2026, SpeakerHub listed a free plan, Silver at USD 149.99 a year and Gold at USD 349.90 a year, with no commission or transaction fees. Prices can change, so check its pricing page.

Do speaker bureaus take new speakers?

Some do. Talarforum, for example, says it is always looking for promising new speakers. But bureaus earn from paid bookings, and some set minimum fees, so it can make sense to build a track record first through open calls and direct outreach.
