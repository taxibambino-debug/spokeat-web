Giving a talk in English when it is not your first language

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

You have been invited to give a guest lecture, a meetup talk or a conference session in English, which is not your first language. You know your subject, but you worry about your accent, losing a word mid-sentence or not understanding a question.

This guide looks at what research says about accent and being understood, and what that means for how you prepare. The short version: your audience needs to understand you, not to hear a native speaker. Those are different goals, and the second one is not required.

Key takeaways

  • Accent and being understood are related but partly separate. Research shows speech can be heavily accented and still highly intelligible.
  • Speaking rate matters. In one set of experiments, listeners rated speech that was neither very slow nor very fast as easiest to understand.
  • Research on whether accent lowers credibility is mixed. An early finding has not held up in several replication attempts.
  • Native speakers are not automatically easier to follow for an international audience. Plain structure helps everyone.
  • Prepare more, cut content, list your key terms, put new terms on slides and rehearse out loud.

Accent is not the same as being understood

Researchers who study second-language speech separate three things [1] [2]:

  • Accentedness: how different your speech sounds from a local variety of English.
  • Comprehensibility: how easy listeners feel your speech is to understand.
  • Intelligibility: how much listeners actually understand, often measured by asking them to write down what they heard.

The best-known study on this is Murray Munro and Tracey Derwing's 1995 paper in Language Learning. Looking back on it in 2022, the authors summarise the key finding as these dimensions being "related, but partially independent", and note that speech "can be heavily accented but highly intelligible" [1]. A 2022 research timeline in Language Teaching describes the same study as showing that listeners could understand second-language speech despite a foreign accent [2].

The finding has been tested again. A 2021 conceptual replication with learners of Spanish reported results that "generally patterned similarly" to the original studies [3].

The limits. These studies use recorded speech samples rated by listeners in a lab, not live talks with slides and a room. They tell you that accent alone does not decide whether you are understood. They do not tell you exactly how a specific audience will react to you.

What makes speech easier to follow

If accent is not the main thing, what is? Two lines of research give useful clues.

Pace

Munro and Derwing tested speaking rate in two experiments. In the second, they slowed down and sped up the same recordings with software, so that only the rate changed. In both experiments, listeners gave the best ratings to speech that was somewhat faster than typical second-language speech. Very fast and very slow speech were both rated lower [4].

The practical point: you do not need to speak very slowly. You need a steady, unhurried pace and clear pauses between ideas. Rushing because you are nervous is the bigger risk. Our guide on calming nerves before a talk covers that.

Words, fluency and stress

A study of 40 French learners of English looked at 19 different speech measures and how they related to ratings of comprehensibility by 60 listeners. For less advanced speakers, vocabulary and fluency mattered most. For more advanced speakers, grammar and how well ideas were connected mattered more. Errors in word stress affected ratings at every level [5].

That suggests three checks:

  1. Know the stress pattern of your key technical terms. A wrong stress on a word you repeat twenty times costs more than an accent.
  2. Join your ideas clearly. Signal where you are going and when you move on.
  3. Choose words you can say fluently over impressive words you hesitate on.

Does an accent make you less credible?

You may have read that people believe statements less when they are said with a foreign accent. This comes from a 2010 study by Shiri Lev-Ari and Boaz Keysar. American listeners rated trivia statements as less true when non-native speakers read them aloud. When listeners were told the study was about how hard speech is to understand, the effect disappeared for mild accents but not for heavy ones [6].

The finding has not held up well. A 2024 paper in the Journal of Cognition lists a direct replication that found no difference between native and foreign accents, another study in Switzerland with the same result, and other studies with partial or mixed support. Its own experiment, with Italian listeners, also found no reliable accent effect on truth ratings. The same paper did find that a separate group of listeners rated foreign-accented speakers lower on some social traits, so bias exists even when it does not change what people believe [7].

What to take from this. Some listeners may judge an accent, but the evidence that it makes your content less believable is weak and contested. Focus on what you control: structure, examples and expertise.

Native speakers are not always easier to follow

A small study in the Journal of Business Communication compared 14 native and 13 non-native English speakers in business negotiation scenarios. Most native speakers tried to adapt their language, but their strategies varied a lot, and the authors point to a gap between how well native speakers understood intercultural communication and how well they could actually adjust [8]. The sample is small, so treat this as a pointer rather than proof.

Speaking clearly to a mixed audience is a skill native speakers have to learn too. You know what it is like to listen in a second language, which helps.

Prepare like a lecturer switching to English

The closest research to your situation comes from university lecturers who had to start teaching in English. In a study of 18 Swedish university lecturers, each gave the same short lecture first in Swedish and then in English a week later [9]. They reported that:

  • preparation took noticeably longer in English;
  • they used fewer jokes, asides and improvised examples, and stuck closer to their plan;
  • they had more hesitations and false starts;
  • when they watched the videos, they were surprised at how similar the two lectures looked.

The study ends with nine recommendations [9]. The most useful for a guest speaker:

  • Prepare more than you would in your own language. Budget extra time.
  • Limit the content. Pick your key ideas and emphasise them. Less is more.
  • Do not translate an existing talk word for word. Think and prepare in English.
  • Make a list of key terms and check how to pronounce them.
  • Put new terms on slides or in a handout. Seeing a word as well as hearing it gives the audience a second chance to catch it.
  • Keep text on slides to a minimum. Use slides to structure the talk, not as a script.
  • If you write a full script, do not read it aloud. Use it to prepare, then speak from notes.
  • Rehearse out loud, ideally to someone.

Our guide on how to prepare a guest lecture covers timing and the brief with the course coordinator, and how to make your talk memorable explains why fewer key points stick better.

Prepare for questions, not only the talk

Questions are often the part non-native speakers fear most, because you cannot script them. Three things help:

  • Write down the ten questions you expect and say your answers out loud in English once.
  • Prepare a few phrases for buying time or checking, for example asking the person to repeat the question, or repeating it back in your own words. Repeating a question also helps the rest of the room, who may not have heard it.
  • Accept that you may not know a word. Describing it in simpler words is normal and usually goes unnoticed.

For pace, pauses and gestures, see our article on voice and body language research.

If nerves are the real problem

For many people, the language is not the main issue. The worry about the language is. If you are generally anxious about speaking in front of groups, read shy, introverted or afraid of speaking. If the fear is severe or affects your work, talk to a doctor or psychologist.

A note for Swedish speakers

If you are Swedish, you are likely more fluent than you feel. Sweden ranked 8th in the 2025 EF English Proficiency Index, with higher scores for reading and listening than for speaking and writing [10]. The index is based on a free online test, and EF itself states that the test-takers are self-selected and not guaranteed to be representative [11]. Treat it as a rough signal, not a measurement of you.

Swedish university audiences are often international: the official admissions service lists 39 universities and university colleges offering English-taught courses and programmes [12].

Frequently asked questions

Will my accent make it harder for people to understand me?

Not necessarily. Research shows accent and intelligibility are partly separate, and speech can be heavily accented but highly intelligible [1]. Pace, word stress and clear structure often matter more.

Should I speak slowly when presenting in English?

Speak at a steady, unhurried pace with pauses, but not very slowly. In experiments, both very slow and very fast second-language speech were rated harder to understand [4].

Should I try to reduce my accent before giving a talk?

For a talk, it is usually a better use of time to check the stress of your key terms, simplify your structure and rehearse. Accent reduction is a long project, and the research suggests it is not the main barrier to being understood.

What if I do not understand a question?

Ask the person to repeat it, or repeat it back in your own words to check. This is normal practice for any speaker and also helps the rest of the audience.
