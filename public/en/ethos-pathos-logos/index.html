The art of rhetoric: ethos, pathos and logos for modern professionals

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

You have been asked to give a guest lecture, sit on a panel or speak to an industry group. You know your subject. The harder question is why anyone in the room should believe you, care, and follow your reasoning.

Aristotle gave that question a structure more than 2,000 years ago: ethos, pathos and logos. The terms are everywhere in presentation advice, often in a simplified form. This guide goes back to the source, explains what each term actually meant, and checks it against modern persuasion research. You get a practical way to use all three, and a clear view of where the evidence is strong and where it is thin.

Key takeaways

  • Aristotle described three kinds of proof that the speech itself provides: the speaker's character (ethos), the audience's state of mind (pathos) and the argument (logos) [1].
  • For Aristotle, ethos is built by the speech, not by your job title [1]. Modern studies find that source credibility does shift opinions, but the effect is often modest, can fade, and matters most when people have no prior view [4][5][6].
  • Emotion works best when it comes with a clear, doable action. A large meta-analysis of fear appeals found a small but reliable effect, stronger when messages explained what people could do [7].
  • Logos for a live audience means short, followable reasoning built on things the audience already accepts [3].
  • Treat the three appeals as a checklist for preparation, not as a formula that guarantees persuasion.

Where ethos, pathos and logos come from

The three terms come from Aristotle's Rhetoric, Book I, chapter 2. In J. H. Freese's 1926 English translation, Aristotle defines rhetoric as "the faculty of discovering the possible means of persuasion in reference to any subject whatever" [2]. He then splits proofs into two groups. Some exist outside the speech, such as witnesses or documents. Others are constructed by the speaker [1].

The constructed proofs come in three kinds. In the same translation, published on the Perseus Digital Library: "The first depends upon the moral character of the speaker, the second upon putting the hearer into a certain frame of mind, the third upon the speech itself, in so far as it proves or seems to prove" [1].

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy calls these three proofs the "methodical core" of the Rhetoric [3].

Ethos: credibility you build in the room

What Aristotle meant

Aristotle says the speaker persuades by character when the speech is delivered so as to make the speaker "worthy of confidence". He adds that "this confidence must be due to the speech itself, not to any preconceived idea of the speaker's character" [1].

That second point surprises many professionals. Your title, employer and LinkedIn following are not ethos in Aristotle's technical sense. They are background. Ethos is what the audience infers from how you speak.

Christof Rapp's entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarises the three things an audience looks for: the speaker should come across as competent, of good character and well disposed towards the audience. If any one of these is missing, listeners may doubt the advice [3].

What modern research adds

Psychologists have studied "source credibility" since the mid-20th century. In a classic study, Hovland and Weiss gave students identical articles attributed to either a high- or low-credibility source. Opinions moved more towards the high-credibility source right after reading. Four weeks later, the gap had narrowed: agreement with the trusted source fell and agreement with the less trusted source rose. The amount of factual information people learned did not differ by source [4].

A later meta-analysis by Kumkale, Albarracín and Seignourel found that credibility effects were smaller at delayed tests than immediately after the message. The effect was strongest, and decayed fastest, when people had no prior attitude and limited ability or motivation to think about the message [5].

More recent work is cautious. A 2023 experiment with a US sample found that a manipulated news source was seen as more or less credible as intended, yet credibility affected opinion change only weakly and inconsistently across issues [6]. The authors note limits, including a fictional source.

The fair summary: credibility matters most when the audience is new to the topic, and it works alongside the content, not instead of it.

How to apply it

For example, a hypothetical head of payments giving a guest lecture might:

  • Show competence through specifics. Explain one real decision and the trade-offs, rather than listing job titles.
  • Show good character through candour. Say what you do not know, and where your industry gets things wrong.
  • Show goodwill through relevance. Tie the talk to what the students need for their course or first job.

Pathos: emotion that serves judgement

What Aristotle meant

Pathos is not about making people cry. Aristotle's point is that emotions change how people judge. Rapp quotes his definition of emotions as "those things due to which people, by undergoing a change, differ in their judgements" [3]. Aristotle analyses specific emotions, such as anger, so that a speaker can understand the conditions under which an audience would feel them [3].

What modern research adds

Fear is one of the most studied emotional appeals. Tannenbaum and colleagues meta-analysed 127 articles with 27,372 participants. Fear appeals had a positive but small effect on attitudes, intentions and behaviours (average d = 0.29). They were more effective when the message included efficacy statements (what people could do), and when the recommended action was a one-off rather than a repeated behaviour. The authors found no conditions under which fear appeals backfired [7]. The lead author also warned in APA's press release that fear appeals are not a cure-all because the effect is small [7].

The practical lesson carries beyond fear: an emotional point lands better when the audience also gets a clear, achievable next step.

How to apply it

  • Name the stake for this audience, not for people in general.
  • Pair concern with action. If you raise a risk, show what someone in the room can do about it.
  • Keep it proportionate. Overstated urgency undermines ethos.

Logos: an argument the audience can follow

What Aristotle meant

Logos covers proof through argument. Aristotle's main tool is the enthymeme, a rhetorical deduction he calls the "body of persuasion" [3]. Enthymemes are built from accepted opinions and adapted to a public audience that is less practised at following long chains of reasoning. They often have fewer premises than formal arguments. Aristotle's other tool is the example, an inductive move from one particular case to a similar one [3].

In practice: start from what your audience already accepts, take a small number of steps, and use a well-chosen case to make the link concrete.

What modern research adds

Researchers have compared statistical evidence with narrative evidence. In a 2024 review, Green and Appel describe the meta-analytic picture as mixed. One meta-analysis found statistical evidence had a stronger influence on beliefs and attitudes, while narratives had a stronger influence on intentions [8]. Facts and stories do different jobs. Use both.

How to apply it

  • State your claim in one sentence before you prove it.
  • Use no more than three supporting steps in a short talk.
  • Give one number that matters and one case that shows it.

Using all three in a guest lecture or panel

Appeal What it is In a guest lecture In a panel Common mistake
Ethos Credibility built by how you speak Explain a real decision and its trade-offs Concede a good point from another panellist Reading out your CV
Pathos Putting the audience in a state of mind that shapes judgement Show what the topic means for the students' next step Name who is affected by the issue Emotion with no action attached
Logos Followable reasoning from shared premises One claim, a few steps, one example A short answer with one reason and one case Long chains of slides with no clear claim

The appeals overlap. A precise, honest example builds ethos and logos at once. A relevant story can carry pathos and logos together; see the science of storytelling in talks for what research says about stories and memory.

Where the evidence is thin

Aristotle's three proofs are an analytic framework, not an experimental finding. Modern persuasion research does not map onto them one to one. Several cautions follow from the studies above:

  • Effects are usually modest and depend on context, such as whether the audience already holds a view [5][6].
  • Credibility effects can fade over weeks [4][5].
  • Much of the research uses written messages, students or online samples. A live talk is a different setting.

Use the framework to prepare. Do not expect it to guarantee a result.

If you want to study rhetoric properly, university rhetoric courses cover the classical texts in depth. For the emotional side of speaking, read how speakers connect with an audience.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between ethos, pathos and logos?

Ethos is persuasion through the speaker's character, pathos through the audience's state of mind, and logos through the argument itself. Aristotle described all three as proofs that the speech provides. Most effective talks use all three together.

Which is most important: ethos, pathos or logos?

Aristotle did not rank them as a fixed order, and modern research does not settle the question either. Credibility matters most when the audience has no prior view, while emotional appeals work better when they come with a clear action. In practice, a weak argument is hard to rescue with credibility or emotion alone.

Does ethos come from my job title?

Not in Aristotle's sense. He wrote that the audience's confidence should come from the speech itself, not from a prior view of the speaker. Your title may open the door, but how you reason, admit limits and show care for the audience builds ethos in the room.

Are emotional appeals manipulative?

They can be, if they distort the stakes or push people without giving them a way to act. Aristotle treated emotion as part of how people judge, not a way around judgement. Research on fear appeals suggests that emotion paired with a realistic action is more effective than emotion alone.
