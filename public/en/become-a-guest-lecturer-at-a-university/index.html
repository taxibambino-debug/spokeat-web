How to become a guest lecturer at a university

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

Many professionals would like to give a guest lecture. Few know who to ask. They write to a general university inbox, hear nothing back, and decide that universities are closed to outsiders.

They are not. Universities invite external speakers in many subjects. But the decision is usually made by the teacher who runs one specific course, and it is made inside a teaching plan that is set well in advance.

This guide explains how that system works, who decides, and how to offer a lecture that a busy teacher can accept. Spoke At exists to do the course-finding part for you, but everything below works on its own.

Key takeaways

  • Guest lectures are usually booked at course level, by the teacher in charge of the course, not by the university centrally.
  • Central routes also exist: alumni offices, career services, business partnership teams and student unions.
  • A good offer is tied to one course and its learning objectives, not to your company.
  • Teaching is planned months ahead, so timing matters as much as the topic.
  • Expect to give your time for free in most cases. Check before you assume otherwise.

How universities actually book guest lecturers

The decision sits with the course

Most university teaching is organised in courses (also called modules or units). Each course has a teacher who is responsible for it. Job titles vary: course coordinator, course organiser, course leader, module leader or module convenor. The University of Exeter, for example, sends draft timetables to "module convenors" for checking [8]. The University of Edinburgh's course catalogue lists a "course organiser" for each course [9].

This is the person who usually invites guests. Aalto University School of Business says it plainly: guest lecturers are often invited by the teacher in charge of a course [2]. RMIT Europe describes the same pattern for online guest lectures. It connects you with a course coordinator in your field, and the two of you agree the format, timing and date [3].

Central routes exist too

Some universities run central schemes that pass offers on to teachers:

Route Who you contact Best for Real example
Course level The course coordinator or module leader A topic that fits one specific course Aalto School of Business [2], RMIT Europe [3]
Alumni office The university's alumni team Graduates of that university Stockholm University [4]
Business partnerships A school's partnerships or corporate relations team Practitioners in business and management Sheffield University Management School [1]
Student union or societies The union's business relations team Lunch talks and company presentations KTH Royal Institute of Technology [5]

Stockholm University's alumni office says guest lecturers who can share real-world experience are in high demand, from students and from course leaders in many departments. It asks alumni to send an idea and promises to try to match them with the right student audience [4]. KTH tells external speakers they can contact its student union or the teacher of a specific programme [5].

Central routes are useful, but they add a step. Your offer still has to land with a teacher who has a course, a topic and a free slot.

Why the course matters more than the university

A course teacher is responsible for learning outcomes. Every session has to help students reach them. That is why Aalto asks guests to agree the content in advance with the responsible teacher, so the lecture meets the course's learning objectives. It also asks guests to describe their company only briefly and to avoid a general company presentation [2].

Sheffield University Management School shows what a good fit looks like. Its past guest talks were each tied to a module: digitalisation in a postgraduate module on intelligent organisations, raising investment for startups in an accounting and finance module for managers, and diversity in leadership in an MBA masterclass series [1].

So the right question is not "Which university should I speak at?" It is "Which course needs what I know?" Our guide on how to find the right university course for a guest lecture shows how to read course catalogues and syllabi to answer it.

What students want from a guest lecturer

Two student surveys give a useful picture. Both have limits: each studied students at a single US institution in a specific field, so treat them as indications, not rules.

A survey of 107 students in strategic communication courses found that 82% were satisfied or highly satisfied with guest speakers. The most wanted topics were career advice (88.3%), networking tips (86.4%) and the speaker's professional journey (85.4%). A topic closely tied to the course was wanted by 72.5%. Students disliked speakers who talked too much about themselves, who talked "at" rather than "with" them, who used text-heavy slides, and who left too little time for questions [6].

A second study surveyed 114 students about industry guest speakers. Of those who had taken a class with one, 86.1% said they liked it. Students valued guests most when the speaker currently worked in a field related to the course content, spoke enthusiastically and honestly, and answered questions. They particularly valued insight into the speaker's day-to-day work and advice on their own career path [7].

The lesson for you: bring current, concrete experience from your field, link it to what the course is teaching, and leave real time for questions.

Timing: work with the teaching year

Universities plan teaching long before it happens. At the University of Exeter, departments had to supply their teaching requirements for the 2026/27 year to central timetabling by 2 April 2026. Draft timetables for the first term went to module convenors on 10 July 2026 [8].

A single guest slot inside an existing session is often easier to fit in than a new timetabled event. But the course structure, reading list and assessments are usually fixed before term starts. If your offer arrives in the middle of a busy teaching period, even a willing teacher may not be able to use it. Our guide to the academic calendar for guest lecturers explains terms, study periods and the best windows to get in touch.

Step by step: how to become a guest lecturer

  1. Define one teachable topic. Pick something you know from current practice and can explain in 30 to 45 minutes. One real case beats a broad overview.
  2. Find courses that need it. Search course catalogues for your topic and read the learning outcomes. See how to find the right course.
  3. Identify the right person. Look for the course coordinator, course organiser or module leader on the course page.
  4. Write a short, specific email. Name the course, the learning outcome you can support, and two or three formats you could offer. Our guest lecture email templates give you a starting point.
  5. Offer flexible formats. RMIT Europe's online guest lectures range from a 15-minute Q&A facilitated by the course coordinator to a 30-minute presentation, live or pre-recorded [3]. Online is often easier for a teacher to schedule. Read more about guest teaching online.
  6. Start where you have a connection. Your own university often has an alumni route. See how to speak at your old university.
  7. Follow up once, then move on. One polite reminder after a week or two is enough. If there is still no answer, try a different course.
  8. Prepare properly once you are booked. See how to prepare a guest lecture students will remember.

What to expect: pay, time and selection

Pay. Do not assume you will be paid. Aalto University School of Business, for example, states that it does not pay a fee to external participants [2]. If a fee or travel costs matter to you, ask before you agree.

Time. A typical slot is short. Sheffield University Management School describes sessions of about 30 to 40 minutes plus 15 to 20 minutes of questions [1]. Add preparation time and a short call with the teacher beforehand.

Selection. Not every offer is taken up. Aalto notes that only a limited number of courses run at a given time, so not every proposal can be used [2]. A "no" or a "not this term" usually reflects the timetable, not your expertise.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Writing to a general inbox when the course page names the person responsible.
  • Offering a company presentation instead of a lecture tied to course content.
  • Pitching a topic that is too broad, such as "the future of finance", when the course covers one part of it.
  • Getting the timing wrong, for example writing during exam weeks or after the course has started.
  • Sending a long CV instead of two lines on why you fit this course.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a PhD to give a guest lecture?

No. Guest lecture schemes are often aimed at practitioners. Sheffield University Management School invites business professionals to share insights from their current roles, and Stockholm University's alumni office asks for alumni with expertise or work experience [1] [4].

Do guest lecturers get paid?

Often not. Aalto University School of Business, for example, says it does not pay external participants [2]. Practice varies between universities, so ask the teacher before you agree if pay or expenses matter to you.

Can I guest lecture at a university I did not attend?

Yes. Alumni routes favour graduates, but course-level routes do not require it. Aalto invites alumni and other corporate representatives, and KTH tells external speakers they can contact the teacher of a specific programme [2] [5].

How long is a typical guest lecture?

It varies by course. Sheffield describes about 30 to 40 minutes plus 15 to 20 minutes of questions, while RMIT Europe's online format ranges from a 15-minute Q&A to a 30-minute presentation [1] [3].

When should I contact a university?

Before the course is planned, not during it. Teaching structures are often fixed months ahead, so read our guide on when to pitch a guest lecture before you write.
