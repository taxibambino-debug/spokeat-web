Build your personal brand by speaking

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

Most advice on how to build your personal brand is about posting more: more LinkedIn updates, more opinions, more visibility. That can work, but it has a weakness. Everything you post is you talking about yourself.

A talk is different. Someone else chose you, put your name on their programme and gave you their audience. That is evidence other people can check.

This article explains what a personal brand is in practical terms, why talks are strong evidence for it, how to choose a topic and keep your profile consistent, and how to tell whether it is working. It is about positioning. If you want to know what speaking does for your authority and career outcomes, read why speaking builds industry authority.

Key takeaways

  • A personal brand is the impression people form of you, based on the evidence they can find. Research on the topic is still young and mostly qualitative.
  • Experiments show that praise from a third party is received better than self-promotion. A talk is a form of third-party endorsement.
  • Research on source credibility suggests a credible messenger matters most when people do not yet know your field or your view.
  • One clear topic, repeated across talks, profile and posts, builds a sharper brand than many scattered topics.
  • Measure it by what others do: invitations, introductions and people quoting your topic back to you.

What a personal brand actually is

A 2018 systematic review in Frontiers in Psychology analysed 100 academic publications on personal branding. The authors define a personal brand as a set of characteristics of an individual, such as attributes, values and beliefs, "rendered into the differentiated narrative and imagery" with the aim of standing out "in the minds of the target audience" [1].

Two parts of that definition matter for professionals:

  • It lives in other people's minds. You can shape it, but you do not own it.
  • It needs a target audience. A brand aimed at everyone is not a brand.

The same review is also honest about the evidence. Of the 100 papers, 34 were conceptual, 42 qualitative, 17 quantitative and 7 mixed-method. The authors describe the field as fragmented, with no commonly accepted definition, and note that studies tend to use small samples from a limited range of industries, mainly in Europe, Australia and North America [1]. The outcomes most often discussed were career advancement and social capital such as influence and visibility [1]. Treat claims that personal branding guarantees a promotion with care.

A practical way to put it: your personal brand is what someone finds when they check you out before a meeting, and what they remember afterwards.

Why talks are stronger evidence than posts

Someone else is vouching for you

There is a well-known problem with promoting yourself. You need to show you are competent, but boasting makes people like you less. Researchers call this the self-promotion dilemma.

In three experiments published in 2006, Jeffrey Pfeffer and colleagues found that when a third party presented someone's achievements, that person made a better impression and others were more willing to help them. The benefit held even when the third party was a paid agent with a clear interest in the person's success [2]. In one study, a job candidate presented by a recruiter was rated more likeable and, in that study, more competent than the same candidate saying the same words himself [3].

The limits. The studies used students and simulated scenarios, such as reading an interview transcript or imagining a book negotiation [3]. They did not study conference programmes or guest lectures. But the mechanism fits: when a university, association or conference introduces you as a speaker, it does the praising for you.

Credibility matters most with new audiences

A 2010 meta-analysis of 54 datasets looked at when a credible source makes a message more persuasive. The effect was strongest when people had no prior attitude and little prior knowledge of the topic. When people already held an opinion, the source mattered less [4]. The authors also found that the effect of credibility faded over about two weeks in the conditions where it was strongest, and they note their findings are correlational [4].

For your brand, that points to two things. First, a credible setting such as a university course or a respected association helps most with people who do not know you yet. Second, one appearance fades. Repetition is what makes it stick.

Buyers say expert content beats sales material

The Edelman and LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report is the most cited survey on this. Its 2025 edition surveyed nearly 2,000 professionals [5]. Among the "hidden buyers" in the survey, people in finance, legal, compliance, procurement or operations who influence purchases without being the main contact, 71% said thought leadership was more effective than traditional marketing or sales material at showing a supplier's potential value [6].

The caveats. The report is produced by a PR firm and LinkedIn, both of which benefit from more thought leadership. The answers are self-reported. And the figures are about companies' content in general, not about individual speakers or talks [5] [6]. Read it as a signal that shown expertise earns attention, not as proof that one talk wins business.

Choose one topic you can own

A brand needs a clear target audience [1]. In practice, that means one topic, phrased so that an organiser could put it in a programme.

Ask yourself:

  1. What do people already ask you about? That is usually your strongest topic.
  2. Who needs to hear it? Students, peers, buyers or senior decision-makers each need a different angle on the same expertise.
  3. Can you say it in one line? For example, a hypothetical head of payments might choose "how instant payments change cash management for mid-sized companies" rather than "fintech".
  4. Can you talk about it for three years? Brands are built through repetition. Our 12-month plan from first talk to keynote shows how one topic grows from a meetup to a larger stage.

A narrow topic feels risky. It is usually the opposite. Organisers look for a speaker on a specific subject, not a generalist.

Make your profile, posts and talks say the same thing

Consistency is what turns separate talks into a brand. When someone moves from your talk to your LinkedIn profile to your company bio, they should find the same topic described in similar words.

Check these points:

  • Headline and summary. Does your LinkedIn headline name your topic, or only your job title?
  • Speaking record. List talks with date, organiser and a link. A programme listing from a third party is stronger than a claim. Our guide to what organisers check in your speaker profile covers what to include.
  • Posts. Post less about yourself and more about the topic. A short summary of what you said in a talk, with the organiser's permission, is both content and evidence. See turn one talk into a month of content.
  • Photos and recordings. These show you in front of an audience. Ask the organiser before you publish anything that shows other people.
  • Bio for organisers. Keep one short and one longer version, both built around the same topic line.

How to measure your personal brand

There is no reliable single score for a personal brand. Follower counts measure reach, not reputation. Better signs are things other people do:

  • Inbound invitations. Are organisers contacting you, rather than the other way round?
  • Introductions. Do people introduce you by your topic ("you should talk to them about…")?
  • Repeat invitations. Did a course coordinator or association invite you back?
  • Search. What comes up when you search your own name with your topic? Programme pages and recordings should appear over time.
  • Conversations. Do clients, colleagues or recruiters mention a talk they saw or heard about?

Keep a simple log with a date for each of these. Over six to twelve months, the trend tells you more than any single number.

What speaking will not do for your brand

  • It will not fix a vague topic. Ten talks on ten subjects leave no clear impression.
  • It will not work once. Credibility effects fade [4]. Plan a series, not a one-off.
  • It cannot replace substance. A brand built on talks with little content will show its gaps quickly.
  • It is not a paid-gig strategy. Do not expect a fee for a guest lecture or a meetup slot. The return is visibility and evidence.

Frequently asked questions

How do I build my personal brand as a professional?

Choose one clear topic, show your expertise in places where others vouch for you (such as talks, guest lectures and panels), and keep your profile consistent with that topic. Repeat this over months rather than relying on one big appearance.

Is public speaking good for personal branding?

Speaking gives you third-party endorsement, which experiments show is received better than self-promotion [2]. There is no study directly measuring the effect of talks on personal brands, so treat this as a well-grounded inference, not a proven result.

Do I need to post on LinkedIn every day?

No. Consistency of topic matters more than frequency. A few posts that build on your talks are stronger evidence than daily posts on scattered subjects.

How long does it take to build a personal brand through speaking?

There is no fixed timeline. Plan for at least six to twelve months of regular talks on one topic before expecting inbound invitations.

What is the difference between a personal brand and authority?

A personal brand is the overall impression people have of you and your topic. Authority is the recognised expertise that grows from it. Read more in why speaking builds industry authority.
