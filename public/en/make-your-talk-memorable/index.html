Why audiences forget talks, and how to make yours stick

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 16 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

You spent hours on a guest lecture. The students listened, asked good questions and thanked you. A month later, most of them would struggle to tell a friend what you said.

That is not a sign you did badly. Forgetting is the normal state of memory. The useful question is what you can do, within one talk, to make the important part last. This article looks at what research on memory, attention and learning shows, where the studies stop applying to a single talk, and how to design a presentation that people remember.

Key takeaways

  • People forget facts from a single exposure, whoever presents them. In one classic study, factual recall dropped noticeably over four weeks [5].
  • Retrieving information, not just hearing it again, is a strong driver of long-term memory in lab studies [2].
  • The "10-minute attention span" is not well supported. Attention seems to vary more with the teacher than with a fixed clock [1].
  • Activity and discussion improve learning in university courses compared with lecturing alone [3].
  • Stories are, on average, remembered better than expository text, and distraction weakens their effect [4][6].

Why audiences forget

Forgetting is normal

In Hovland and Weiss's classic 1951 study, students read articles and answered factual questions right afterwards and again four weeks later. The average number of factual questions answered correctly fell from about 3.3 to about 2.6. The source of the article, trusted or not, made no significant difference to how much was learned [5].

The lesson is simple: even attentive people lose much of what they take in once. A talk is usually a one-time exposure.

Hearing is not the same as remembering

Karpicke and Roediger studied what helps people retain information for a week. Repeated testing during learning improved retention compared with repeated studying. In a second experiment, restudying items people had already recalled did not help, while repeatedly recalling them improved later retention substantially [2]. Their conclusion: repeatedly retrieving information is central to long-term retention.

These were lab studies with word lists, not talks. But they point to a gap in most presentations: the audience hears everything once and is never asked to pull anything back out.

Attention is not a fixed 10-minute clock

You will often hear that attention collapses after 10 to 15 minutes. Bradbury's 2016 review in Advances in Physiology Education found that the claim rests mostly on secondary citations and "scant few primary investigations". The studies that do measure attention often have methodological flaws. The available data do not support a fixed limit, and the biggest variation in attention seemed to come from differences between instructors, not from the lecture format itself [1].

That is good news. How you teach matters more than a stopwatch.

Distraction eats into absorption

Research on narrative "transportation" shows that distraction and secondary tasks reduce how absorbed people become in a story [6]. Phones, dense slides and side conversations all compete for the same attention.

How to make your talk stick

1. Decide what you want remembered

Pick one core message and two or three supporting points. If you cannot say the message in one sentence, the audience will not be able to either. Say it near the start and again at the end.

2. Make the audience retrieve, not just listen

Build in moments where people have to recall or predict. For example, a hypothetical cybersecurity lead giving a guest lecture might pause after 15 minutes and ask: "Which of the three risks I described applies to your group project?" At the end, ask people to write down the one thing they will use.

This applies the retrieval findings above [2]. It is a reasonable extension, not a tested talk format.

3. Add activity and discussion

Freeman and colleagues meta-analysed 225 studies of undergraduate science, engineering and maths teaching. Exam performance was about 0.47 standard deviations higher under active learning, and students in traditional lecture courses were about 1.5 times as likely to fail [3]. They define active learning as engaging students "through activities and/or discussion in class".

Those were whole courses, not single guest lectures. Still, even a short pair discussion or a show-of-hands question gives people something to do with what you said.

4. Carry key ideas in a story

A 2021 meta-analysis found that stories were remembered better than expository texts, with a moderate average effect, though most studies tested reading soon afterwards [4]. Use a short, specific case with a goal, a cause and a consequence for the point you most want remembered. See the science of storytelling.

5. Remove competition for attention

Because distraction reduces absorption [6], put less on slides. Blank the screen when you tell the key story or make the main point. Ask the organiser whether people tend to use laptops in the session, and plan around it.

6. Make the structure easy to follow

Tell people where you are going and signal each transition. In a brain-imaging study of storytelling, listeners who understood the speaker showed activity that anticipated the speaker's [7]. The study was small, but it fits a practical rule: help the audience predict what comes next.

7. Give a reason to retrieve it later

If you are a guest in a course, ask the course coordinator whether your topic links to an assignment or exam question. A follow-up task is another retrieval opportunity [2]. Our guide on how to prepare a guest lecture covers how to agree this with the coordinator.

Where the evidence stops

Most of these studies were not run on one-off talks by visiting professionals. They used word lists, written texts, or semester-long courses. The direction of the findings is consistent: retrieval, activity, structure and focus support learning. The size of the effect in your 45-minute guest lecture is unknown. Treat the advice as well-grounded design principles, and check what works by asking your audience afterwards.

Frequently asked questions

How much of a presentation do people remember?

There is no reliable single percentage for talks. Studies show that people forget a good share of information from a single exposure within weeks. What they remember depends on structure, attention, and whether they use the information again.

Is the 10-minute attention span real?

The evidence for a fixed 10 to 15 minute limit is weak. A review found the claim relies mostly on secondary sources and flawed studies. Attention appears to vary more with the teacher than with a set time.

What is the best way to end a presentation so people remember it?

Restate your core message in one sentence and ask the audience to recall or apply it, for example by writing down one thing they will do. Retrieval research suggests that pulling information back out helps it last. Avoid ending on a dense final slide.

Do stories make talks more memorable?

On average, yes. A meta-analysis found stories were remembered better than expository texts. Most of the studies used reading and short delays, so use stories to carry key points rather than replacing all explanation.

Should I hand out slides so people remember more?

Handouts can help people review later, but they do not replace retrieval. If you share material, add a question or task that asks people to use it. That gives them a reason to recall your talk.
