Speaking while employed: employer approval and outside-activity rules

Written by Ted Scheiman, banking and fintech executive, guest lecturer. Last reviewed 17 November 2026. How we research and fact-check

Most people who give guest lectures or conference talks have a day job. Their expertise comes from that job. That is exactly why the question matters: does your employer need to know, and can it say no?

This guide explains the rules that most often apply, with examples from Sweden, the UK and the US. It covers the difference between speaking as yourself and speaking for your employer, confidentiality, fees, and gives you a checklist and a short email to send your manager.

This is general information, not legal advice. Your contract, collective agreement and internal policies decide what applies to you. If in doubt, ask your HR department, your union or an employment lawyer.

Key takeaways

  • Public employers often have written rules on outside activities. In Sweden, the Public Employment Act bans side activities that could damage trust in impartiality.
  • Private employers usually rely on the employment contract, collective agreements and internal policies. Competing with your employer is the classic risk.
  • Speaking for your employer is part of your job. Speaking as yourself is an outside activity. Agree which one it is before you accept.
  • Fees raise the stakes. Some employers ban or restrict payment for talks related to your job.
  • A short, early email to your manager solves most problems before they start.

Speaking for your employer or as yourself?

Start by deciding which of these it is.

On behalf of your employer. You represent the company, use its name and title, and speak in work time. This is part of your job. Your employer has a say in the message, and you should agree with it who receives any fee.

As yourself, about your field. You speak in your own time, as an expert. Your employer may still care, because the audience will link you to your job title, and because you may draw on things you learned at work.

As yourself, about something unrelated. A talk about your hobby is rarely an issue, unless your employer has a general rule that all outside activities must be reported.

Many problems come from a mix of the first two: a talk in your own name, using your job title, about your employer's work. Clear that up with your manager first.

Public sector: Sweden

In Sweden, public employees are covered by the Public Employment Act (lagen om offentlig anställning, LOA). Section 7 says an employee may not have any employment, assignment or activity that could undermine trust in their own or another employee's impartiality at work, or that could damage the authority's reputation [1]. Swedish law calls this a "förtroendeskadlig bisyssla", a side activity that damages trust.

The Act also sets out the process [1]:

  • The employer must inform employees about the kinds of circumstances that can make a side activity impermissible (section 7 a).
  • On request, the employee must provide the information the employer needs to assess their side activities (section 7 b).
  • The employer must decide that an employee stops, or does not take up, a side activity that breaks section 7. The decision must be written and give reasons (section 7 c).

The Swedish Agency for Government Employers (Arbetsgivarverket) explains that state employers must or may also stop two other types: side activities that hinder work, and competing side activities. These are regulated in the state's collective agreements [2]. It also notes that managers, and university teachers for side activities linked to their subject area, have a duty to report [2].

Universities apply this to fees too. Uppsala University's FAQ says a side activity should normally not create business transactions between the university and the employee, so a consultant fee should normally not be paid to a company the employee runs [3].

Public sector: UK and US examples

UK civil service. The UK government's guidance on outside interests in the Civil Service says "All remunerated outside employment, work and appointments should be declared, whether or not considered relevant." Where a role might affect someone's work, they should get line manager approval before taking it up [4]. Any actual conflict of interest must be resolved in a way that removes it [4].

US federal employees. Federal ethics rules say an employee may not receive compensation from any source other than the government for teaching, speaking or writing that relates to their official duties [5]. There is an exception for teaching a course that is part of the regular curriculum of a college or school. Employees also may not use their official title to identify themselves when speaking, except as one of several biographical details [5].

If you work in another public body, look for its code of conduct or policy on outside interests.

Private sector

Private employers rarely have a statute like LOA behind them. The rules usually come from three places: your employment contract, any collective agreement, and internal policies such as a code of conduct or a communications policy.

Collective agreements can be specific. The joint commentary on a Swedish white-collar agreement in the engineering sector (Teknikavtalet, 2020–2023) is one example. It says employees should consult their employer when it comes to assignments or side activities "of a more extensive kind", that the aim is to avoid conflicts of interest, and that what harms the job must be judged case by case [6]. It also says the demands for loyalty are higher the more senior the employee is [6]. Check which agreement, if any, applies to you; terms differ between sectors and versions.

Competing with your employer is the clearest risk. The union Engineers of Sweden says in its employment FAQ that your own business "must not compete with your employer operations" [7]. A paid workshop that sells the same advice your employer sells to clients is a much bigger issue than a free guest lecture to students.

Confidentiality and communications policies

Even an unpaid talk can break rules if it reveals things it should not. Before you write your talk:

  • Remove client names, figures and internal examples unless you have permission.
  • Check whether your employer has a policy on public statements, media or social media, and whether talks fall under it.
  • Ask whether you may use the company name, logo or job title, and whether a disclaimer ("my own views") is expected.
  • If the talk will be recorded and published, say so when you ask. A recording reaches more people than the room.

Fees

Money changes the picture. A fee makes the talk look like paid outside work, and some employers treat all paid work as something to declare, as the UK civil service guidance shows [4]. If you speak on behalf of your employer, agree in advance who receives any fee.

If you invoice in Sweden while employed, you may need FA-skatt. Skatteverket says you must then refer to your F-skatt approval in writing for each assignment [8]. Our guide do guest lecturers get paid? covers fees, expenses and invoicing in more detail.

Checklist before you say yes

  1. Is this on behalf of my employer, or as myself?
  2. Does my contract, collective agreement or code of conduct mention outside activities?
  3. Could the talk compete with what my employer sells?
  4. Could anyone see a conflict of interest, for example a supplier or client in the audience?
  5. Will I use my job title or the company name?
  6. Does the talk include anything confidential?
  7. Is there a fee, and who should receive it?
  8. Is it in work time or my own time?
  9. Will it be recorded or published?

If several answers are "yes" or "not sure", ask before you accept.

A short email to your manager

Subject: Guest lecture at [university] on [date]

Hi [name],

I have been invited to give a [45-minute] guest lecture on [topic] in the course [course name] at [university] on [date]. It is [unpaid / paid with a fee of X]. I would do it [in my own time / during work hours, if that is fine].

I will speak about [general topic] and will not share client names or internal figures. I plan to use my job title in the introduction. Please let me know if that works, or if there is anything you would like me to avoid.

Thanks, [your name]

Frequently asked questions

Do I need my employer's permission to give a talk?

It depends on your employer and your role. Public employers often have written rules, and in Sweden they can ban side activities that damage trust in impartiality. Private employers usually rely on your contract, any collective agreement and internal policies. Asking early is the safest route.

Can my employer stop me from speaking in my own time?

In some cases. In Swedish public employment the employer must stop side activities that damage trust, and state employers can also stop activities that hinder work or compete. In the private sector, competing with your employer is the clearest risk. Check your contract and ask HR or your union.

Can I keep a speaking fee while employed?

Sometimes. If you speak on behalf of your employer, agree in advance who receives the fee. Some employers ban or restrict fees for talks related to your job, such as US federal ethics rules. Check your policy and declare paid work where required.

What is a conflict of interest when speaking?

A conflict arises when the talk could affect, or appear to affect, your judgement at work. Examples include speaking for a supplier you buy from, or selling advice that competes with your employer.
